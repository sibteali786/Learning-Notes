Transition Metals: the 4s / 3d Twist

AP Chemistry · Atomic Structure · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem The Aufbau order tells you WHAT, not WHY

Standard Aufbau order (4s before 3d) always gives the correct final electron configuration — but it implies 4s is lower energy than 3d, which is only true up through calcium. Starting at scandium, the real energy ordering flips, and two elements (chromium, copper) break the "expected" pattern entirely. This video untangles what's actually true vs. what's just a useful shortcut.

Setup K and Ca — the "normal" cases

K  (19 e⁻) → [Ar] 4s¹
Ca (20 e⁻) → [Ar] 4s²
Ca²⁺ (18 e⁻) → [Ar]  — loses both 4s electrons
Here 4s really is lower energy than 3d — no surprises yet.

The Twist At Scandium, the Energy Order Flips

Counterintuitive but real
Past calcium, 4s becomes higher energy than 3d — the opposite of what the Aufbau diagonal "implies." Yet electron configurations are still written with 4s listed before 3d (e.g. Sc = [Ar] 4s² 3d¹) because that's the order they fill in during buildup — it just doesn't reflect final relative energy.
How we know this: ionization evidence
When scandium loses an electron to form Sc⁺, the electron removed comes from 4s, not 3d: Sc⁺ = [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹. Since ionization always removes the highest-energy electron first, this proves 4s is higher energy than 3d in scandium — even though 4s filled "first."

Exceptions Chromium & Copper Break the Pattern

Most transition metals follow the expected fill pattern smoothly (Sc through V, then Mn through Ni). But two elements steal an electron from 4s to make 3d "extra stable":

The "half-filled / fully-filled is extra stable" explanation
A commonly cited (but incomplete) reason: half-filled (3d⁵) and fully-filled (3d¹⁰) subshells have a special stability. This holds for Cr and Cu specifically, but isn't a universal rule — the real explanation involves complex multi-electron interactions beyond the scope of general chemistry.

Practical Tips

  • For writing configurations: trust the standard Aufbau order, but memorize Cr and Cu as exceptions — they're common exam traps.
  • For transition metal ions: electrons are removed from 4s before 3d, regardless of write order. E.g. Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶ (both 4s electrons gone first), not [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴.
  • Don't over-explain "half-filled/full stability" as a hard rule — it fits Cr/Cu but isn't predictive elsewhere.
  • Remember: the order electrons are written in ≠ the order they're removed in for transition metals — this is the single most-tested nuance in this topic.
