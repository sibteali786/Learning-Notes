Ionization Energy: Three Competing Factors

AP Chemistry · Periodic Trends · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem Why does ionization energy drop going down a group?

Ionization energy (IE) = energy needed to remove one electron from a neutral gaseous atom. Always positive (it always costs energy to fight the nucleus's pull), measured in kJ/mol.
ElementIE₁ (kJ/mol)
H1312
Li520
Na496
K419
Clear group-1 trend: IE decreases going down the group. This video breaks down exactly why using three competing physical factors.

Factor 1 Nuclear Charge (alone would ↑ IE going down)

More protons = stronger pull on electrons = you'd expect it to be harder to remove an electron.
H: +1 nuclear charge   Li: +3 nuclear charge
Looking at nuclear charge in isolation, Li's higher charge (+3 vs +1) suggests Li's outer electron should be held more tightly than hydrogen's — predicting a higher IE for Li. But that's not what we observe. Something else must be counteracting it.

Factor 2 Electron Shielding (alone would ↓ IE going down)

Inner-shell (core) electrons repel outer-shell electrons, partially canceling the nucleus's pull.
Lithium's two inner electrons shield the outer one
Li's 2 core electrons (in 1s²) push back against the nucleus's attraction on the outer 2s¹ electron. Looked at alone, this shielding effect makes it easier to remove Li's electron — predicting lower IE.
Zeff = Z − S   (S = number of shielding/core electrons)
AtomZCore (shielding) electronsZeff (simple estimate)
H101 − 0 = 1
Li323 − 2 = 1
Nuclear charge and shielding roughly cancel
By this simple estimate, H and Li's outer electrons both feel Zeff ≈ +1 — almost identical! (A more precise calculation gives Li's actual Zeff ≈ 1.3, slightly higher than this rough estimate, but still much closer to H than the raw +3 nuclear charge would suggest.) Since factors 1 and 2 mostly cancel out, they can't explain the IE trend by themselves — we need the third factor.

Factor 3 Distance (the decisive factor)

Coulomb's law is distance-dependent: closer charges attract more strongly.
AtomOuter electron's distance from nucleusAttractive force felt
HClose (1st shell)Strong → hard to remove → high IE
LiFarther (2nd shell)Weaker → easier to remove → lower IE
This is what actually drives the group trend
Since nuclear charge and shielding roughly cancel each other, distance is left as the deciding factor. Going down a group, the outer electron sits in a shell farther from the nucleus each time → weaker attraction → progressively lower ionization energy. This matches the observed H → Li → Na → K trend perfectly.

Worked Example Full Ionization Picture

H (1s¹) + 1312 kJ/mol → H⁺  (0 electrons left)
Li (1s² 2s¹) + 520 kJ/mol → Li⁺ (1s²)  (outer 2s electron removed)
Notice Li⁺ keeps its full 1s² inner shell — only the outermost, most weakly held electron is removed.

Key Distinctions

Nuclear charge (Z)↑ Z → ↑ IEMostly cancelled by shielding
Electron shielding (S)↑ shielding → ↓ IEMostly cancelled by nuclear charge
Distance from nucleus↑ distance → ↓ IEDominant factor for the group-down trend

Practical Tips

  • Don't just say "IE decreases down a group" — be ready to explain the three-factor reasoning: nuclear charge and shielding roughly cancel, so distance decides.
  • Zeff = Z − S is a simplified model. It's good for building intuition, but the real value (like Li's ~1.3) is usually a bit higher than the naive calculation.
  • Ionization always removes the highest-energy (most weakly held, generally outermost) electron first — same rule used for writing ion configurations.
  • This same three-factor framework (nuclear charge, shielding, distance) reappears for explaining atomic radius and electron affinity trends — worth internalizing once, reused everywhere.
