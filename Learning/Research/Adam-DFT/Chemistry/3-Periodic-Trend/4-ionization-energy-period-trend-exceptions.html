Ionization Energy Across a Period — Two Exceptions

AP Chemistry · Periodic Trends · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem The period trend isn't a smooth increase

Going left-to-right across Li → Ne, ionization energy generally increases due to rising Zeff. But the real data has two dips — Be→B and N→O — that break the pattern. Understanding why reveals a deeper layer of orbital-level detail beyond simple Zeff.

Baseline Why IE Generally Rises Across a Period

Zeff = Z − S  (S = shielding/core electrons)
AtomConfigZCore e⁻ (S)Zeff (simple)Zeff (actual)
Li1s² 2s¹32+1~1.3
Be1s² 2s²42+2~1.9
Same number of core (shielding) electrons for both, but Be has one more proton — so Be's outer electrons feel a stronger net pull. Stronger pull → harder to remove → higher IE (900 vs 520).
Why actual Zeff differs from the simple estimate
The crude formula treats all core electrons as perfect shields (S = integer). In reality shielding is partial, and other valence electrons contribute a small amount too — which is why Be's actual Zeff (~1.9) is a bit lower than the naive +2 estimate.

Exception 1 Be → B Dip (900 → 800)

B (5 e⁻) → 1s² 2s² 2p¹
Boron's 5th electron goes into a 2p orbital, which is higher energy (and farther out, on average) than the 2s orbital.
Extra shielding from the 2s pair
Be's electron configuration is fully in the 2s subshell (2s²). Boron adds one electron to a *different*, higher-energy 2p orbital. The two 2s electrons sit closer to the nucleus and partially shield the more distant 2p electron. That extra shielding makes B's outermost electron easier to remove than expected — despite B having one more proton than Be.

Exception 2 N → O Dip (1402 → 1314)

N (7 e⁻) → 1s² 2s² 2p³  (each 2p orbital singly occupied)
O (8 e⁻) → 1s² 2s² 2p⁴  (one 2p orbital now has 2 electrons)
Electron-electron repulsion in a paired orbital
Nitrogen has one electron in each of its three 2p orbitals (Hund's rule, no pairing needed). Oxygen's 4th 2p electron must pair up with one already there. Two electrons crammed into the same orbital repel each other (same charge) — this repulsion makes it easier to remove one of that pair, despite oxygen having one more proton than nitrogen.

Key Distinctions

Be → B2p electron shielded by 2s pair, plus higher orbital energySmall IE decrease

General pattern to remember
Both dips occur right when a subshell configuration shifts unfavorably: going from an empty-to-singly-filled p subshell (B) or from half-filled-stable to now-pairing (O). Half-filled (p³) and fully-filled (p⁶) subshells tend to be extra stable reference points — dips tend to occur right after crossing them.

Practical Tips

  • Overall period trend: IE increases left→right due to rising Zeff — memorize this as the default.
  • Memorize the two standard exceptions: group 2→13 (like Be→B) and group 15→16 (like N→O) — these are heavily tested.
  • Both exceptions trace back to subshell-level detail (2s vs 2p energy, or orbital pairing) — not to Zeff itself decreasing.
  • Quick mnemonic: dips happen right after a "comfortable" half-filled or fully-filled state is disturbed by the next added electron.
