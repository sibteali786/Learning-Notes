Isotopes & Mass Spectrometry

AP Chemistry · Atomic Structure and Properties — visual notes

Why The Problem

Every atom of an element has the same number of protons, but the number of neutrons can vary. That means atoms of the same element can have different masses — these variants are called isotopes. Chemists needed a way to (1) name/notate these variants, (2) figure out how much of each variant actually exists in nature, and (3) use that to compute the single "atomic mass" value printed on the periodic table. Mass spectrometry is the experimental technique that makes this possible.

Core Isotopes & Notation

Isotope = atoms of the same element (same # protons) with a different # of neutrons → different atomic mass.

The word comes from Greek iso- ("same") + -topos ("place") — isotopes sit at the same spot on the periodic table.

# neutrons = mass number − # protons

Example: Carbon-12 → mass number 12, atomic number 6 → 12 − 6 = 6 neutrons.
Chromium-52 → mass number 52, atomic number 24 → 52 − 24 = 28 neutrons.

Unit The Unified Atomic Mass Unit (u)

Working in kilograms for atom-sized masses is unwieldy (protons ≈ 1.673×10⁻²⁷ kg). So chemists defined 1 u = exactly 1/12 the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom. In these units, protons and neutrons both have a mass very close to 1 u, and electrons are almost negligible (≈ 5.486×10⁻⁴ u).

Compare Key Distinctions

Mass Number

  • Whole number: # protons + # neutrons
  • Used in notation: e.g. carbon-12
  • No units

Atomic Mass

  • Actual measured mass of one specific isotope
  • Expressed in u
  • Close to, but not identical to, mass number

Average Atomic Mass

  • Weighted average across all naturally occurring isotopes
  • This is the number on the periodic table
  • Also called "atomic weight" (unitless when shown that way)

Formula Average Atomic Mass = Weighted Average

average atomic mass = Σ (relative abundance × atomic mass)ᵢ
Worked Example — Chlorine

Cl-35: 75.76% abundant, atomic mass 34.97 u
Cl-37: 24.24% abundant, atomic mass 36.97 u

(0.7576 × 34.97 u) + (0.2424 × 36.97 u)
= 26.49 u + 8.96 u
= 35.45 u ✓ matches periodic table

Because Cl-35 is ~3× more abundant, the weighted average lands much closer to 35 than to 37 — this is the whole point of "weighted."

Diagram Anatomy of an Atom

Diagram How a Mass Spectrometer Works

Sample → vapor → ions → accelerated → bent by a magnetic field by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) → detector counts each ion type

  • Detector measures two things: the m/z ratio for each ion, and how many ions hit it at that ratio.
  • Output = mass spectrum: relative abundance (y-axis) vs. m/z or atomic mass (x-axis).
  • If every ion has charge z = 1+, the x-axis simplifies to just atomic mass in u.
  • Peak height = relative abundance of that isotope. Tallest peak = most common isotope.

Example Real-World Scenario — Zirconium

Scenario

A geologist runs a pure zirconium sample through a mass spectrometer to determine its average atomic mass — the same process used to certify reference materials or verify a sample's elemental identity.

Zr-90 dominates the spectrum (tallest peak, 51.45%), which is why the average lands closest to 90–91 rather than the midpoint of the full range.

Scenario

A geochronologist measures the ratio of Chlorine-36 (a rare, radioactive isotope with a ~301,000-year half-life) versus stable Cl-35/Cl-37 in groundwater or rock samples — mass spectrometry lets them date when that water was last exposed to the atmosphere.

Tips Interview / Practical Tips

Tip Relative abundances of all stable isotopes of an element always sum to 100%.
Tip Don't round intermediate steps in weighted-average calculations — round only the final answer, or you'll drift off by hundredths.
Tip Deflection in the magnetic field is inversely proportional to m/z — heavier ions (or lower charge) deflect less.
Tip The masses printed on the periodic table are technically relative atomic masses (average atomic mass ÷ 1 u), which is why they're unitless.
Tip If two isotopes are roughly equally abundant, the average atomic mass will land roughly halfway between their masses — a fast sanity check before crunching the full formula.
