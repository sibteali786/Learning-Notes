Ways to Represent a Molecule

Name → Empirical → Molecular → Structural formula

Problem Why Multiple Representations?

A molecule's name alone (e.g. "benzene") tells you almost nothing about its composition. Chemists needed increasingly detailed ways to describe what a molecule is actually made of and how it's put together — each level of formula adds more information than the last, at the cost of being more complex to write.

Core The Four Levels of Detail

1. Name

  • e.g. "benzene"
  • No compositional info

2. Empirical Formula

  • Simplest whole-number ratio of elements
  • From "empirical" = based on observation
  • Benzene: CH (1 C : 1 H)

3. Molecular Formula

  • Actual count of each atom in one molecule
  • Benzene: C₆H₆
  • Can reduce back to the empirical ratio

4. Structural Formula

  • Shows how atoms are bonded/arranged
  • Can include 3D shape info
  • Reveals rings, double bonds, etc.

Key Distinction When Empirical = Molecular

Empirical and molecular formulas aren't always different — they're only different when the molecular counts can be reduced to a simpler ratio (like benzene's C₆H₆ → CH).

Example — Water

Water has exactly 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen per molecule. The ratio (2:1) already matches the actual atom count, so its empirical formula and molecular formula are the same: H₂O.

Tips Practical Tips

Tip Going from molecular → empirical is always a one-way simplification: divide all subscripts by their greatest common factor.
Tip In organic chemistry skeletal structures, unlabeled vertices are carbons, and any "missing" bonds (carbon needs 4 total) are implicitly filled with hydrogens.
Tip Don't assume empirical ≠ molecular — always check if the molecular ratio is already in simplest form (like water) before reducing.
