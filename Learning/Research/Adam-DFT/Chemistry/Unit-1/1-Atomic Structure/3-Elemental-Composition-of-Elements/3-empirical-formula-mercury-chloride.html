Empirical Formula from % Composition

Worked example — Mystery molecule: 73% Hg, 27% Cl

Problem What Are We Solving?

A mystery molecule is measured to be 73% mercury by mass and 27% chlorine by mass. Using only percent composition and a periodic table (average atomic masses), determine the likely empirical formula — the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in the molecule.

Core The Trick: Assume 100 g

Since we only have percentages, assume the sample is 100 g total. This turns each percentage directly into grams — 73% → 73 g Hg, 27% → 27 g Cl — making the moles calculation clean. Any assumed mass works (5 g, 1000 g), but 100 g avoids extra arithmetic.

Formula Grams → Moles

moles = mass (g) × [1 / molar mass (g/mol)]
ElementAssumed massMolar mass (g/mol)Moles
Mercury (Hg)73 g200.5973 ÷ 200.59 ≈ 0.36 mol
Chlorine (Cl)27 g35.4527 ÷ 35.45 ≈ 0.76 mol

Only 2 sig figs kept for the mole values since the original mass percentages (73%, 27%) only had 2 sig figs.

Diagram Moles → Simplest Ratio

2 × 0.36 ≈ 0.72, close enough to 0.76 — empirical analysis rarely gives exact ratios

Result

The simplest whole-number ratio is 1 Hg : 2 Cl, giving the empirical formula HgCl₂. This compound is mercury(II) chloride.

Compare Empirical Formula Analysis — Key Ideas

  • Moles here directly represent the relative number of atoms (via Avogadro's number) — so a mole ratio is an atom ratio.
  • Empirical analysis from real experimental data is rarely exact — round to the nearest sensible whole-number ratio, don't chase perfect decimals.
  • The assumed sample size (100 g, 5 g, 1000 g) never changes the final ratio — percentages define ratios, not absolute amounts.

Tips Practical Tips

Tip Always divide every element's mole count by the smallest mole value in the set — this normalizes one element to exactly 1.0 and reveals the ratio.
Tip If a ratio comes out close to a simple fraction (like 2.11 ≈ 2, or 1.5 → multiply everything by 2), round to the nearest whole number or scale up — real lab data won't be perfectly clean.
Tip Track significant figures from your original percentages — they cap how many sig figs your mole values (and thus confidence in the ratio) can have.
