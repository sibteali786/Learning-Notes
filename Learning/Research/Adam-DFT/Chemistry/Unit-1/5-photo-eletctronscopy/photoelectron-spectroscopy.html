Photoelectron Spectroscopy (PES)

AP Chemistry · Unit 1 · Reading electron configuration from a spectrum

BINDING ENERGY PEAK HEIGHT = e⁻ COUNT ELECTRON CONFIG Zeff / SHIELDING LINK

📌 Problem Statement

Given only a graph (a "photoelectron spectrum") with no element name attached, how do you figure out exactly which element it is — and its full electron configuration — just from the shape of some peaks?

PES works because every subshell has a distinct binding energy, and the height of each peak tells you exactly how many electrons live in that subshell.

⚙️ How It Works

1. The Setup

Atom stream →
←
← High-energy X-ray photons

Photons collide with atoms and knock electrons out (like tiny fireworks 💥). Ejected electrons fly into a magnetic field that deflects them toward a detector.

  • Core electrons (close to nucleus) → high binding energy → most photon energy used just to knock them free → little leftover kinetic energy → don't travel far
  • Valence electrons (far from nucleus) → low binding energy → most photon energy converts to kinetic energy → travel far, hit detector at greater distance

2. Reading the Axes

AxisRepresentsDirection
X — Binding energy (MJ/mol)How tightly held the electron isPlotted right → left, decreasing (1000 → 0)
Y — Relative # of electronsPeak height = # electrons in that subshellTaller peak = more electrons detected there
The x-axis runs backwards — leftmost peaks are the highest binding energy (innermost, 1s), not the lowest.

3. SVG: Matching Peaks to Subshells (Calcium)

Peak heights follow the subshell capacities: s-peaks (2s, 3s, 4s) are always shorter than adjacent p-peaks (2p, 3p) which hold 3× as many electrons (6 vs 2).

4. Build the Configuration, Peak by Peak (left → right = decreasing binding energy = Aufbau order)

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s²

Add up the electrons: 2+2+6+2+6+2 = 20 electrons → neutral atom with 20 protons → Calcium (Ca).

Valence electrons = the peak with the lowest binding energy (furthest right / smallest x-value) — for Ca that's the 4s² peak.

🔍 Key Distinctions

Calcium (Z = 20)

4s² peak is full height (2 electrons) → equal height to other filled s-peaks.

Potassium (Z = 19)

Identical pattern except 4s peak is only half height (1 electron, not 2) — and every peak shifts slightly right (lower BE) because K has one fewer proton pulling on the shells.

Why K's peaks shift right vs. Ca's

Fewer protons in the nucleus (19 vs. 20) → weaker pull on every shell → lower binding energy for every subshell, not just the valence one. Same shape, whole spectrum nudges toward lower binding energy (right on the graph).

🌍 Real-World / Exam Connection

PES ↔ First Ionization Energy

The binding energy of the outermost (valence) peak is exactly your first ionization energy — the energy to remove the easiest electron.

You can only read first ionization energy this way. Once you remove one electron, all the remaining electron-electron interactions change, so the original spectrum no longer tells you the 2nd or 3rd ionization energies.

🎯 Practical / Exam Tips

  • Read the x-axis right-to-left in your head if it helps — leftmost peak = 1s, always the tallest binding energy.
  • Peak height ratios are your best sanity check: p-subshells should be ~3× the height of same-row s-subshells (6 vs 2 electrons).
  • Sum all peak electron counts → gives you Z (atomic number) for a neutral atom → identifies the element.
  • Furthest-right peak = valence electrons = first ionization energy.
  • Comparing two adjacent elements (like K vs Ca): same peak pattern, but fewer protons → whole spectrum shifts to lower binding energy, plus the outermost peak's height drops if that subshell isn't full.
