⚙️ How It Works

0. What is PES? (Definition)

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) is an experimental technique that measures the relative energies of electrons in atoms and molecules. A sample is bombarded with high-energy UV or X-ray radiation, ejecting electrons ("photoelectrons"); their kinetic energies are recorded by an energy analyzer and counted by a detector. Scientists use it to study elemental composition and bonding — here, we use it to confirm electron shells, subshells, and electron configurations.

1. The Physics: Deriving BE = hν − KE

PES is built on Einstein's photoelectric effect (1905): light of sufficient energy striking a surface ejects electrons.

By conservation of energy, the ionizing photon's energy must be split between (a) overcoming the electron's binding energy and (b) whatever's left over becomes the ejected electron's kinetic energy:

Ephoton = BE + KEelectron

The energy of a photon is given by Planck's relation:

Ephoton = hν

(h = Planck's constant, 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s; ν = frequency in Hz)

Substituting and rearranging to solve for BE:

hν = BE + KEelectron → BE = hν − KEelectron
hν is fixed (you chose the radiation source). KE is what the detector measures directly. BE is calculated from the two — and that calculated BE is exactly the x-axis value of each peak in the spectrum.

2. Reading Conventions

  • X-axis: binding energy, decreasing left → right, and logarithmic scale (not linear!) — units MJ/mol or eV.
  • Y-axis: relative electron count → peak height ∝ number of electrons in that subshell.
  • Electrons in the same shell cluster into a "group" of peaks with similar BE (e.g., 2s and 2p are close together, both much lower BE than 1s).

3. Worked Example — Lithium (1s² 2s¹)

Two peaks, 2:1 intensity ratio. The taller/closer-to-origin peak (higher BE) = 1s² (2 electrons, less shielded, closer to nucleus). The shorter/farther peak = 2s¹ (1 electron).

For Li, the 2s peak's BE equals the first ionization energy exactly — it's the outermost/least-bound electron. But the 1s peak's BE is not the 2nd ionization energy — once you remove the 2s electron, the 1s electrons get pulled in tighter and their BE changes.

Grouping check: 2s and 2p sit close together (both between 1–10 MJ/mol) since they're the same shell — while 1s sits far off alone (~100 MJ/mol). That "2 peaks close, 1 far" pattern is your visual shell/subshell signal.

5. Concept Check — Predicting Peak Count (Calcium)

You don't need a spectrum to predict how many peaks an element will show — just count occupied subshells in its electron configuration.

Ca: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² → 6 occupied subshells → 6 peaks

6. Identifying a Mystery Element — Aluminum

5 peaks → 5 subshells: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p. Intensities (L→R): 2×, 2×, 6×, 2×, 1×.

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹ → Al

The giveaway is the tiny rightmost peak — only half the height of 1s/2s/3s → just 1 electron in 3p → first element of the 3rd-row p-block → aluminum.

Cross-check: 3 "groups" of peaks visible (⟨1s⟩, ⟨2s,2p⟩, ⟨3s,3p⟩) → matches 3 occupied electron shells in Al. Everything lines up.

🔍 Key Distinctions

Core electrons

Higher BE · closer to nucleus · less shielded · left side of spectrum · large groups isolated from valence peaks.

Valence electrons

Lowest BE · outermost · most shielded · rightmost peak(s) · BE of this peak = first ionization energy.

🎯 Exam Tips

  • Memorize BE = hν − KE; you may be asked to compute BE given hν and KE directly.
  • The x-axis is logarithmic and reversed — always double check which way BE increases before reading peaks.
  • Use peak grouping (clusters close together = same shell) to sanity-check subshell assignments, not just individual heights.
  • Only the outermost (lowest BE) peak's value equals the 1st ionization energy — never assume other peaks correspond to any ionization energy.
  • Total electrons summed across all peaks = atomic number Z (for a neutral atom) → identifies the element.
