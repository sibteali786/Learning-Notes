Valence & Core Electrons

AP Chemistry · Atomic Structure · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem Why bother writing electron configurations at all?

The whole point of electron configurations isn't the notation itself — it's that they predict how an atom reacts. Not all electrons matter equally for reactivity: only the outermost ones do. Splitting an atom's electrons into valence (reactive, outer) vs core (stable, inner) is what makes electron configuration actually useful.

Definitions Valence vs Core

TermMeaningReactivity role
Valence electronsElectrons in the outermost shellDetermine how the atom bonds/reacts
Core electronsAll electrons in inner (filled) shellsGenerally not involved in reactions

Worked Example Oxygen

O (8 e⁻) → 1s² 2s² 2p⁴
CategoryCountWhere
Core electrons21s² (inner, filled shell)
Valence electrons62s² 2p⁴ (outermost shell)
Why 6 valence electrons matters
Atoms are most stable with a full outer shell (filled s + p subshells = 8 electrons, the octet rule). Oxygen has 6 and wants 2 more — so it grabs 2 electrons from other atoms during reactions. This is exactly why oxygen readily forms bonds (e.g. pulling electrons from hydrogen to form H₂O).

Worked Example Calcium

Ca (20 e⁻) → [Ar] 4s²
CategoryCountWhere
Core electrons18[Ar] — argon's full, stable configuration
Valence electrons24s²
Why calcium loses electrons instead of gaining them
Calcium has only 2 valence electrons. To reach a full outer shell it would need 6 more (hard) — but it's much easier to just lose the 2 it has. This is why calcium readily ionizes to Ca²⁺, exposing the already-full argon-like core underneath.

Practical Tips

  • Valence electrons = electrons in the highest (outermost) shell only — not the total electron count.
  • Core electrons = everything below the outermost shell; treat these as "locked in," non-reactive.
  • Elements with 1–3 valence electrons (metals) tend to lose electrons → form positive ions (cations).
  • Elements with 5–7 valence electrons (nonmetals) tend to gain electrons → form negative ions (anions).
  • Group number = valence electron count, but only reliably for s-block and p-block elements.
  • Noble gas notation is a fast way to isolate valence electrons: everything inside [X] is core, everything after it is valence.
