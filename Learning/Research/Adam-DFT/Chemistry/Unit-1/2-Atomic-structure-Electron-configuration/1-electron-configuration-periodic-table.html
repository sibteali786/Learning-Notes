Electron Configuration & the Periodic Table

AP Chemistry · Atomic Structure · Source: Khan Academy

Problem Why does this matter?

Memorizing element names doesn't tell you how an element behaves. The periodic table is a filing system — an element's position tells you its electron configuration, which predicts its reactivity: will it bond, lose electrons, gain electrons, or stay inert?
Core idea
Reactivity = pattern of an atom's electrons. The periodic table is organized specifically to expose that pattern at a glance.

Model 1 The Bohr Model — Shells

Proposed by Niels Bohr (1913). Electrons orbit the nucleus in circular shells (energy levels), like planets around the sun. Shells closer to the nucleus = lower energy.
ShellMax electrons
1n2
2n8
3n18 (but only 8 needed for stability — see orbitals below)
Octet Rule
Most atoms important to biology/chemistry are stable with 8 electrons in their outermost (valence) shell. Full valence shell = low reactivity.

Pattern Periodic Table = Shell + Column Map

Row (Period) → which shell is being filled.
Column (Group) → number of valence electrons (and thus reactivity).
GroupValence electronsBehaviorExamples
11Loses 1 e⁻ → becomes +1 ionH, Li, Na
144Shares electrons (covalent bonds)C, Si
177Gains 1 e⁻ → becomes −1 ionF, Cl
188 (or 2 for He)Full shell → inert / noble gasHe, Ne, Ar
Real-world scenario: table salt
Sodium (Na, group 1, 1 valence e⁻) desperately wants to lose that lone electron. Chlorine (Cl, group 17, 7 valence e⁻) desperately wants one more electron to complete its octet. Na hands its electron to Cl → Na⁺ and Cl⁻ form and stick together electrostatically → NaCl, table salt. This is exactly why group number predicts reactivity.

Model 2 Orbitals — The More Accurate Picture

The Bohr model is a useful simplification but electrons don't actually circle the nucleus in neat rings. Instead they occupy orbitals: 3D regions of space where an electron is most likely (90% of the time) to be found. Each orbital holds up to 2 electrons. Shells are made of subshells, and subshells are made of orbitals:

1 s orbital + 3 p orbitals shown together — this is the shape of a filled 2n shell.

Reference Electron Configurations

H  → 1s¹
He → 1s²
Li  → 1s² 2s¹
Ne → 1s² 2s² 2p⁶

Fill order: lowest energy orbital first (1s before 2s before 2p...). This is why Period 1 has only 2 elements (H, He) — only 1s is available.

Why is Argon stable with only 8, not 18, electrons?
The 3n shell's d orbital is much higher energy and doesn't start filling until Period 4. So for Period 3 elements, filling just 3s + 3p (8 electrons total) is already the lowest-energy stable state — satisfying the octet rule even though 3n could technically hold 18.

Practical Tips

  • Row number = highest shell being filled (Period 2 → 2n is filling).
  • Group number (1, 14, 17, 18 especially) = valence electron count → predicts ion charge or bonding style directly.
  • Metals (left side, e.g. group 1) tend to lose electrons → form cations (+).
  • Nonmetals (right side, e.g. group 17) tend to gain electrons → form anions (−).
  • Noble gases (group 18) = full valence shell = essentially unreactive. Great "stable reference point" when reasoning about why other elements react the way they do.
  • When writing electron configurations, fill in order of increasing energy: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p...
