Atomic & Ionic Radii

AP Chemistry · Periodic Trends · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem Atoms don't have a fixed edge — so how do we measure them?

An atom isn't a hard sphere — it's a nucleus surrounded by a fuzzy electron probability cloud with no clean boundary. Chemists work around this with a practical trick: measure between two identical bonded atoms, then take half.
atomic radius ≈ (distance between two identical bonded nuclei) ÷ 2

Trend 1 Down a Group — Radius Increases

Going from H → Li (down group 1), an extra shell is added farther from the nucleus. More "stuff," farther out → larger atom.
Simple takeaway
Adding a new outer shell always increases radius — this is the most intuitive of the trends.

Trend 2 Across a Period — Radius Decreases

This one is less obvious: you're adding electrons left-to-right, yet the atom gets smaller.
Li vs Be — same shell count, different nuclear pull
Li has 3 protons (+3), Be has 4 protons (+4). Both have 2 core electrons in the inner shell doing the same amount of shielding. But Be's higher nuclear charge pulls its outer electrons in harder, since the shielding didn't increase to compensate. Result: Be is smaller than Li, despite having one more electron.
Force on outer electronSourceEffect
AttractionPositively charged nucleusPulls outer electron inward
Repulsion (shielding)Inner-shell (core) electronsPushes outer electron outward
Why outer electrons don't shield each other
Outer-shell electrons are all roughly the same distance from the nucleus, so they don't meaningfully block the nuclear pull on each other. Only inner (core) electrons provide real shielding.

Ionic Radius Cations Shrink

Li → Li⁺ + e⁻
Losing the outer electron leaves the same +3 nuclear charge pulling on fewer, now-innermost electrons. No more outer shell to occupy → the ion shrinks noticeably compared to the neutral atom.
General rule
Cation (positive ion) is always smaller than its neutral parent atom.

Ionic Radius Anions Grow

Cl (neutral) → [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵  (7 valence e⁻)
Cl⁻ (anion) → [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶  (8 valence e⁻)
Adding an electron makes the ion larger than the neutral atom — the opposite of cations.
The explanation is genuinely debated
The common textbook explanation: with 8 electrons instead of 7 in the outer shell, there's more electron-electron repulsion, pushing the cloud outward. This is a reasonable intuition, but some chemists dispute it as the full/correct mechanism, and no clean alternative has fully replaced it. For exam purposes, just remember the outcome: anions are larger than their neutral atom.

Key Distinctions

Down a groupIncreasesNew outer shell added
Across a period (L→R)DecreasesNuclear charge increases, shielding stays constant

Neutral → anionIncreasesExtra electron added, more electron-electron repulsion (commonly cited reason)

Practical Tips

  • Atomic radius definition (half the internuclear distance between two identical bonded atoms) is worth memorizing verbatim — it's a common short-answer question.
  • When comparing sizes across a period, always frame it as "same shielding, more nuclear charge" — that's the reasoning graders look for.
  • Cation smaller, anion larger than the parent neutral atom — a quick two-word rule that covers most exam questions.
  • Ionic radius questions get trickier for larger charge differences (e.g. 2+ vs 3+) — the core logic (more charge pulling on fewer electrons = smaller) still applies, just scaled.
