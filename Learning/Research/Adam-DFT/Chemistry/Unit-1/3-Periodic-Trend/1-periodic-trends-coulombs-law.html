Periodic Trends via Coulomb's Law

AP Chemistry · Periodic Trends · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem One equation explains almost every periodic trend

Instead of memorizing separate rules for atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity, all of them fall out of a single idea: Coulomb's Law — the force between charges.
F ∝ (q₁ × q₂) / r²
Here q1 = effective nuclear charge felt by an electron, q2 = electron's charge (constant), r = distance from nucleus to that electron. Everything below is about how q1 (Zeff) and r change across the table.

Core Concept Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)

Zeff ≈ Z − (# core electrons)
Core electrons sit between the nucleus and the valence electrons, shielding valence electrons from the full nuclear pull. Zeff is the "net" positive charge a valence electron actually feels.

Why group 1 elements all "feel" similar
Li and Na have very different atomic numbers, but both land at Zeff ≈ 1. Core electrons scale up right alongside the nucleus down a group — so the net pull on the valence electron stays roughly constant. This is why elements in the same group react similarly.

Extremes
Helium = smallest atom (top-right). Francium = largest atom (bottom-left). Overall trend: radius shrinks as you move up-and-right.

Trend 2 Ionization Energy

Energy needed to remove the first (outermost/valence) electron. High when Coulomb force is strong → high Zeff + low radius = same corner as smallest atoms.
Highest ionization energy: top-right (noble gases, halogens)
Makes intuitive sense: noble gases are already stable and "don't want" to lose an electron. Halogens are one electron away from a full octet, so removing one is costly too.
Lowest: bottom-left (e.g. francium)
One valence electron, far from the nucleus, heavily shielded by many core electrons (low Zeff despite huge atomic number) → easy to remove.

Trend 3 Electron Affinity

Energy released when an electron is added to a neutral atom. High electron affinity = atom "wants" an extra electron = strong Coulomb attraction = high Zeff + low radius → same top-right region, with one exception.
Noble gas exception
Even though noble gases have high Zeff, they don't want extra electrons (already full shell) — so the true electron-affinity "hot zone" is the top-right excluding noble gases, especially halogens like F and Cl.
Sign convention gotcha
"High electron affinity" in casual usage means "releases a lot of energy." But by convention, released energy is recorded as a negative number. Don't let the negative sign confuse you into thinking it means "low affinity."

Trend 4 Electronegativity

How strongly an atom hogs a shared pair of electrons in a bond — closely related to (and follows the same trend as) electron affinity, but conceptually different.

Practical Tips

  • Almost every trend question reduces to: "is Zeff high or low here, and is r large or small?" Work it out from Coulomb's law rather than memorizing arrows.
  • Zeff ≈ atomic number − core electron count. Quick to compute from any electron configuration.
  • Remember the noble-gas carve-out for electron affinity/electronegativity — they buck the "top-right is highest" pattern because they don't want extra electrons at all.
  • Ionization energy and electron affinity are "mirror" concepts: one is about removing an electron, the other about adding one — but both are maximized by the same high-Zeff, low-radius conditions.
  • Watch the sign convention: negative electron affinity values usually mean energy was released, i.e. a strong (high) affinity — not weak.
