First vs. Second Ionization Energy

Why removing electron #2 costs way more than #1 — Lithium as the case study

Problem Why does IE jump so much?

Lithium's first ionization energy is +520 kJ/mol, but its second is +7,298 kJ/mol — over 14× larger. Same nucleus, wildly different cost. Understanding why explains which ions elements actually form (e.g. why Li forms Li⁺, never Li²⁺).

Setup The Lithium electron configuration

Li has atomic number 3 → 3 protons, 3 electrons (neutral) → config 1s² 2s¹
  • 1s² — 2 inner-shell electrons, close to nucleus
  • 2s¹ — 1 outer-shell (valence) electron, far from nucleus

How it works Removing electron 1 vs electron 2

Step 1 (IE₁): Remove the lone 2s electron → charge balance 3p⁺ − 2e⁻ = +1 → forms Li⁺ (1s²).
Step 2 (IE₂): Remove one of the 1s electrons → 3p⁺ − 1e⁻ = +2 → forms Li²⁺ (1s¹).

Key Distinctions The 3 factors driving the gap

Factor1st electron removed (2s¹)2nd electron removed (1s, from Li⁺)
Nuclear charge+3 protons (same both times)+3 protons (same both times)
ShieldingShielded by 2 inner 1s electrons → Zeff ≈ +1.3No inner electrons left to shield it → feels nearly full +3
DistanceIn 2nd energy level — farther from nucleusIn 1st energy level — much closer to nucleus
ResultWeak attraction → easy to remove (520 kJ/mol)Strong attraction → very hard to remove (7,298 kJ/mol)
Quick Zeff formula: Z_eff = Z − (shielding electrons). For the 2s electron in Li: 3 − 2 = +1 (actual ≈ +1.3 with more precise calculations).

Real-World Scenario

This is exactly why Group 1 metals (Li, Na, K...) form +1 ions, never +2. Their single valence electron is easy to strip (low IE₁), but the next electron sits in a full, closer-in noble-gas-like shell — removing it costs an enormous amount of energy. It's also why table salt is Na⁺Cl⁻ and never Na²⁺Cl₂: sodium simply "stops" losing electrons after the first, because IE₂ for Na is similarly gigantic.

Practical Tips

  • A big jump between consecutive ionization energies tells you exactly where the "core" electrons begin — that's how you identify an element's typical ion charge / group number.
  • Always check: did the electron just removed come from a new, lower shell? If yes, expect a huge IE jump.
  • Don't confuse "more protons" with "more effective pull" — after ionization, proton count doesn't change; what changes is shielding and distance.
