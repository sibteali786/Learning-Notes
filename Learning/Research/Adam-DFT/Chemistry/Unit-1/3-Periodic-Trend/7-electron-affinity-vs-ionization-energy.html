Electron Affinity vs. Ionization Energy

Two sides of the same electron-attraction coin — plus a Zeff shielding correction

Problem Two processes, opposite direction

Ionization Energy (IE): energy needed to remove an electron from a neutral atom.
Electron Affinity (EA): energy change when an electron is added to a neutral atom.

Both describe how strongly the nucleus attracts an electron — IE via removal, EA via addition.

How it works Lithium: IE vs EA

IE: pulling the valence e⁻ away fights the nucleus's attraction → costs energy (+).
EA: the incoming e⁻ is still pulled in by the nucleus → releases energy (−), as long as there's real attraction left.

Key Distinctions

Ionization EnergyElectron Affinity
DirectionRemoves e⁻Adds e⁻
Sign conventionAlways positive (endothermic)Usually negative (exothermic) — but not always
Period trendClean, consistent increase L→RGeneral increase L→R, but many exceptions
Key exceptionsFew (mainly 4s/3d nuances)Full subshells (Be, Ne) and half-filled p³ (N)

Exceptions in EA Why Be, N, Ne break the trend

  • Be (1s²2s²) → adding e⁻ forces it into a new, higher-energy 2p orbital → poor attraction → EA ≈ 0 (slightly endothermic)
  • Ne (1s²2s²2p⁶) → adding e⁻ forces it into a whole new shell (3s) → Zeff ≈ 0 on that electron → EA ≈ 0, no affinity (explains noble gas inertness)
  • N (1s²2s²2p³, half-filled p) → added e⁻ must pair up in an already-occupied p orbital → extra electron-electron repulsion → weaker-than-expected EA

Correction Why F has such a large (very negative) EA

Common mistake: For F (1s² 2s² 2p⁵), shielding = 4 (the 2s²2p³ electrons already there), so Zeff = 9 − 4 = +5, and that's why EA is large.
Correct simple-model logic (Z − S, video's convention): shielding electrons are only the inner, complete shell — same-shell electrons are not counted as core shielders.
ElementConfigCore shielders (S)Zeff = Z − SEA (kJ/mol)
Li1s² 2s¹2 (the 1s² core)3 − 2 = +1−60
F1s² 2s² 2p⁵2 (the 1s² core)9 − 2 = +7−328
F's incoming electron lands in the same shell (n=2) as the 2s²2p⁵ electrons already there — those aren't "shielding" in this model, they're same-level electrons. Only the tight, inner 1s² core actually screens the nucleus. A small core (2 e⁻) + large nuclear charge (+9) = a big Zeff (+7) pulling hard on the incoming electron → large energy released → very negative EA.
Caveat: Z − S with "only inner shell counts" is a simplification used for quick visual reasoning. Real Slater's rules give same-shell electrons partial shielding credit (~0.35 each), so F's actual Zeff on its 2p electrons is closer to ~+5.1–5.8, not a clean +7. The direction of the reasoning — small core, high Z, big Zeff, big EA — is still correct; it's the precision that's approximate.

Real-World Scenario

This Zeff logic is exactly why halogens (F, Cl, Br, I) are the most reactive nonmetals — a small, efficient core and a strong pull on incoming electrons makes them eager to grab one more electron and complete their octet, forming stable −1 anions (F⁻, Cl⁻) in salts like NaF and NaCl.

Practical Tips

  • When estimating Zeff by hand, only subtract electrons from completed inner shells — don't count same-shell (valence) electrons as shielders in the quick model.
  • EA ≈ 0 is your signal for: newly-opened shell/subshell (Be, Ne) or a shell that's now completely full (noble gases).
  • A dip at N mid-period is your signal for: electron pairing repulsion in a half-filled subshell.
  • For exam-level precision beyond this quick model, mention Slater's rules — same-shell electrons contribute partial (not zero) shielding.
