Electronegativity vs. Electron Affinity

Two closely related ideas — one general, one bond-specific

Problem Two similar-sounding terms

Both describe how much an atom "wants" electrons, but at different scales:
  • Electron Affinity — a general property: how much an isolated atom likes gaining an electron (energy released when adding one).
  • Electronegativity — a specific case: within a covalent bond, how badly an atom hogs the shared electrons, pulling them to spend more time near itself.
High EA ⇄ high electronegativity — they track together, but electronegativity only applies when atoms are actually bonded and sharing electrons.

How it works Water (H₂O) as the classic example

Both H atoms want to "borrow" an electron to feel like He (2 valence e⁻ = stable). O wants to borrow 2 electrons to feel like Ne (8 valence e⁻ = stable). They share — but O hogs the shared pairs more than H does, since O is more electronegative. Result: partial negative charge (δ−) on O, partial positive charge (δ+) on each H. This is why water is polar and behaves the way it does (hydrogen bonding, solvent properties, etc).

Periodic Trends

Across a period (left → right): ↑ more electronegative
Na has 1 valence e⁻ → happy to give it away (low IE, low pull on electrons).
Cl is 1 electron short of a full shell → desperately wants to gain one → hogs electrons hard.
Down a group (top → bottom): ↓ less electronegative
Atom size increases → outer electron sits farther from nucleus, more shielding in between → weaker pull on any shared/incoming electron. Cs barely holds its own electron, let alone someone else's.
Overall trend: electronegativity increases toward the top-right of the periodic table (excluding noble gases, which rarely bond). Most electronegative real bonding atoms: F, O, N, Cl. Least electronegative ("electropositive"): bottom-left, e.g. Cs, Fr.

Real-World Scenario

Electronegativity differences predict bond type and reactivity: a large gap (like Na–Cl, ΔEN ≈ 2.1) gives an ionic bond — electrons transfer completely. A small/zero gap (like C–H or Cl–Cl) gives a covalent bond with even or near-even sharing. This is the backbone of predicting oxidation states and reaction mechanisms in organic chemistry — e.g. why carbonyl carbons (C=O) are electrophilic: oxygen hogs the electron density, leaving carbon partially positive and open to nucleophilic attack.

Practical Tips

  • Same trend logic as ionization energy — think "how close is this atom to a stable noble-gas configuration?" Nearly-full shells (halogens) hog electrons; nearly-empty shells (alkali metals) give them up.
  • Noble gases are usually excluded from electronegativity trend discussions since they don't typically form bonds.
  • Don't confuse the two terms on exams: EA = single-atom energy value; electronegativity = relative, bond-context pulling power.
