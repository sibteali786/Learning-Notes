Comparing Diatomic Bonds H₂ · N₂ · O₂

Same axes, three molecules — how bond order and atomic radius shape the curve D3.js curves

Problem Statement

Given three unlabeled potential-energy-vs-distance curves for H₂, N₂, and O₂ — figure out which curve belongs to which molecule, using only two clues: bond order and atomic radius.

H–H
single bond
N≡N
triple bond
O=O
double bond

How It Works

Two rules decide both the depth (bond energy) and the horizontal position (bond length) of each well:

  • BOND ORDER ↑ → deeper well (more bond energy) and shorter equilibrium distance. Triple > double > single.
  • ATOM SIZE ↓ → shorter equilibrium distance, independent of bond order. Smaller atoms sit closer together.
  • TIE-BREAKER → when the two rules conflict (as with N vs O, where O atoms are slightly smaller but N₂ has the higher bond order), bond order wins — it dominates over the small radius difference.
H₂ — single bond, smallest atoms
N₂ — triple bond, deepest & shortest well
O₂ — double bond, in between
💡 Reading order: Rank by well depth first (deepest = highest bond order = N₂), then by how far left the minimum sits (further left = shorter bond = more compact molecule). Don't rank by depth alone — O₂'s well is shallower than N₂'s but still much deeper than H₂'s, matching its double bond.

Higher bond order → shorter bond length and higher bond energy — this is the dominant trend to lean on.
⚠️ Watch out: Atomic radius trends (increase down a column, decrease across a row) would predict O atoms are slightly smaller than N atoms — tempting to guess O₂'s well sits left of N₂'s on radius alone. But bond order effects are stronger: N₂'s triple bond pulls the nuclei closer than O₂'s double bond despite N being the (very slightly) larger atom.

Real-World Example Scenario

🧪 Scenario: This is exactly why N₂ is so chemically inert — its triple bond has a huge bond energy (945 kJ/mol), so breaking it (e.g., industrially, in the Haber process for ammonia) requires high pressure, high temperature, and a catalyst. Meanwhile O₂'s weaker double bond (498 kJ/mol) is part of why oxygen is so much more reactive — it's "easier" to break apart and form new bonds with, like in combustion.

Practical / Interview Tips

  • Two-step ID strategy: (1) rank well depths → assign by bond order (deepest = highest order); (2) use minimum position only to double-check, since bond order usually dominates radius effects.
  • If asked to justify N₂ over O₂ for the deepest well: cite bond order (triple vs. double) as the primary driver, atomic radius as a secondary, usually-overridden factor.
  • Remember the "trumps" hierarchy: bond order effect > atomic radius effect when the two point in different directions for neighboring elements.
