Coulomb's Law & Lattice Energy

Why does NaCl, RbCl, and MgF₂ take different amounts of energy to pull apart? D3.js chart

Problem Statement

Ionic compounds like table salt (NaCl) form a repeating 3D lattice of alternating cations and anions, held together by electrostatic (Coulomb) attraction. Lattice energy is the energy needed to pull all the ions infinitely far apart — the ionic-bond equivalent of covalent bond energy.

The question: given a few ionic compounds, how do you predict which has the highest lattice energy?

How It Works

F ∝ q₁q₂
r²

Coulomb's Law: the force between two ions scales with the product of their charges (q₁, q₂) and inversely with the square of the distance between their nuclei (r).

  • CHARGE ↑ → stronger attraction → higher lattice energy (a 2+/2− pair pulls much harder than a 1+/1− pair).
  • DISTANCE ↓ → smaller ions sit closer together → stronger attraction → higher lattice energy.
  • Opposite charges attract, same charges repel — this is what holds the alternating lattice pattern together.

💡 Two levers, same direction: Going from NaCl → RbCl, only the cation radius changes (charges stay +1/−1) — smaller Na⁺ wins on distance alone. Going from NaCl → MgF₂, both the charge (2+ instead of 1+) and the radii (both ions smaller) push lattice energy up — which is why MgF₂'s lattice energy is dramatically higher, not just a little.

Real-World Example Scenario

🧪 Scenario: This is why table salt (NaCl) dissolves easily in water at room temperature but compounds like MgF₂ or MgO are used in high-temperature refractory materials (furnace linings) — their huge lattice energies mean it takes enormous heat (or a very polar solvent with strong ion-dipole interactions) to pull the ions apart. Charge, more than size, is usually the biggest lever on lattice energy.

Practical / Interview Tips

  • Two-factor checklist for ranking lattice energy: (1) charge magnitude (dominant factor — squares/products of charge swing energy the most), (2) ionic radius (secondary — smaller ions = shorter r = stronger force).
  • When comparing same-charge compounds (e.g., NaCl vs RbCl), radius is the only variable — smaller cation wins.
  • When charge changes too (e.g., NaCl vs MgF₂), expect a much bigger jump — charge effects compound with radius effects rather than just adding to them.
  • Remember: lattice energy and covalent bond energy are conceptually the same idea (energy to separate to infinity) — just applied to ionic vs. covalent bonding.
