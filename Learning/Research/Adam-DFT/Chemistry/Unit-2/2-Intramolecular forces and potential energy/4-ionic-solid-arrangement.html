Ionic Solids — How Ions Arrange Themselves

Checkerboard vs. clumped vs. random — and why relative ion size matters (RbBr example)

Problem Statement

Given a 1:1 mix of cations and anions forming a solid crystal, what's the actual 2D "particulate model" slice going to look like? Would ions segregate into blocks, arrange randomly, or settle into a checkerboard pattern?

And separately: when drawing two different ions (like Rb⁺ and Br⁻), how do you decide which circle to draw bigger?

How It Works

Ions arrange to maximize attractive forces (opposite charges near each other) and minimize repulsive forces (like charges far apart) — straight from Coulomb's law. The checkerboard pattern is the only one of the three layouts below that achieves this everywhere.

Segregated
✗ Like charges bunched together — maximizes repulsion
Random
✗ No consistent pattern — leaves some like-charge pairs adjacent
Checkerboard
✓ Every ion is surrounded only by opposite charges — real crystal structure
💡 Rule of thumb: Every cation's nearest neighbors are anions, and vice versa — that's the defining feature of an ionic lattice, not just a stylistic choice.

Key Distinctions — Sizing Ions Correctly (RbBr)

When drawing a particulate model, ion size matters — it's not just decorative. Compare Rb⁺ and Br⁻:

● Br⁻ (larger)    ● Rb⁺ (smaller)
⚠️ Don't overdo the size gap: Rb⁺ and Br⁻ are isoelectronic (same electron count) and differ by only 2 protons — draw them as close in size, with Rb⁺ just slightly smaller. Save dramatic size differences for pairs with very different electron shell counts (e.g., Mg²⁺ vs. O²⁻).

Real-World Example Scenario

🧪 Scenario: This checkerboard packing principle is exactly what X-ray crystallographers rely on to identify unknown ionic compounds — the diffraction pattern reveals a repeating unit cell, and the ratio + spacing of bright spots tells you the ion sizes and charges, letting you reconstruct which cation/anion pair (and what structure, like rock-salt vs. fluorite) you're looking at, without ever "seeing" the atoms directly.

Worked Practice Problem

Question: Two particulate diagrams show SrO and an unknown MO. In SrO, Sr²⁺ is drawn smaller than O²⁻. In MO, M²⁺ is drawn the same size as O²⁻. Which metal is M?

Choices
AMg²⁺
BTi²⁺
CFe²⁺
DBa²⁺ ✓

Step 1 — Why is Sr²⁺ smaller than O²⁻ despite both having a full outer p-shell?
They're not isoelectronic: Sr²⁺ (Z=38, −2e⁻) still has electrons through the 4th shell (Kr configuration), while O²⁻ (Z=8, +2e⁻) only fills through the 2nd shell (Ne configuration). Different shell counts dominate — Sr²⁺'s extra shell would normally make it bigger, but here it's drawn smaller, so the diagram is testing something else.

⚠️ Common trap: It's tempting to reach for "same electron count → same size" (the isoelectronic-series logic from Rb⁺/Br⁻ above). But electron count alone isn't the full story — Mg²⁺ is isoelectronic with O²⁻ (both 10 electrons, Ne configuration), yet Mg's 12 protons vs. O's 8 protons would pull that shared shell in much tighter, making Mg²⁺ noticeably smaller than O²⁻, not equal. So "same size" in the diagram rules Mg out, not in.

Step 2 — Use the group trend instead. Sr, Mg, and Ba are all Group 2 (same charge, same general chemistry). Moving up the group (Sr → Mg) removes a shell → cation gets smaller → widens the gap with O²⁻ (wrong direction). Moving down the group (Sr → Ba) adds a shell → cation gets bigger → closes the gap with O²⁻ (right direction, matching the diagram).

Sr²⁺ < O²⁻ (given)  →  need a bigger Group 2 cation  →  Ba²⁺ (one period down) ≈ O²⁻
💡 Takeaway: When a diagram gives you a size relationship, don't just check "same electron count" — check whether the trend direction (shells added/removed relative to a known reference ion) actually explains the size you're shown. Ti²⁺ and Fe²⁺ are transition metals with more complex shielding and weren't even needed here, since the Group 2 trend already nails it.

Practical / Interview Tips

  • Any question asking "would this ionic arrangement be stable?" → check whether like charges are adjacent. If yes, it's not the real structure.
  • To compare ion sizes quickly: same electron count (isoelectronic) → more protons = smaller ion (stronger nuclear pull on the same shell).
  • When protons AND electron shells both differ (e.g., comparing across periods), shell count usually dominates — more shells = bigger, regardless of protons.
  • In particulate models, size differences should be proportionate to the actual radius gap — small proton differences (2–3) → subtle size difference; full extra electron shells → dramatic size difference.
