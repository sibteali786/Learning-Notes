Lewis Diagrams: Double Bonds

Worked through CH₂O (formaldehyde) — what to do when single bonds leave the central atom short
Problem
Sometimes drawing all single bonds uses up every valence electron, but the central atom still isn't at 8. Formaldehyde (CH₂O) is the classic case: single bonds alone leave carbon 2 electrons short. This walks through the fix — converting a terminal atom's lone pair into a second bond.
How It Works

Worked Example: CH₂O (Formaldehyde)

  1. Account for valence electrons — sum each atom's valence e⁻ (multiply by count).
  2. Draw single bonds first, central atom = least electronegative, non-H. Subtract from the total.
  3. Allocate remaining e⁻ to terminal atoms as lone pairs, chasing the octet rule.
  4. Check the central atom. If it's short of 8, steal a lone pair from a terminal atom and turn it into a second bond (single → double bond).
Electron Count
C: 4 v.e.
H: 1 v.e. × 2 = 2
O: 6 v.e.
Total = 4 + 2 + 6 = 12 valence e⁻
Electron Bookkeeping
12 → 3 single bonds drawn (C–O, C–H, C–H) (−6) → 6 left
6 → all given to O as 3 lone pairs (−6) → 0 left
Check C: only 6 e⁻ around it (3 bonds) → short of octet!
The Fix
O has 3 lone pairs → 1 lone pair becomes a bonding pair → C=O double bond forms → C now has 8 e⁻, O still has 8 e⁻ (2 lone pairs + 4 shared)
Step 2 — all single bonds, C only has 6 e⁻ (short)
Step 4 — one O lone pair → second bond. C=O double bond. Both atoms at 8.
Key Distinctions
MoleculeAll single bonds enough?Fix needed
SiF₄Yes — Si hits octet with 4 single bondsNone
CH₂ONo — C stuck at 6 e⁻1 lone pair on O → C=O double bond
CO₂ (for reference)No — C stuck at 4 e⁻2 lone pairs (one per O) → two C=O double bonds

Rule of thumb: only atoms with lone pairs can donate one to form a new bond — hydrogen never has lone pairs, so it can never be part of a double bond.

Real-World Example Scenarios
Scenario 1 — Formalin & preservation: Formaldehyde (CH₂O) dissolved in water is "formalin," used to preserve biological specimens and, historically, embalming. Its reactivity (it cross-links proteins) traces directly back to that polar C=O double bond — the same double bond this diagram derives from first principles.
Scenario 2 — Predicting shape from the diagram: Because carbon here has 3 bonding groups (2 H, 1 O) and zero lone pairs, VSEPR theory predicts a trigonal planar shape — this is exactly why formaldehyde is a flat molecule, a fact you can read directly off the completed Lewis diagram before doing any geometry.
Interview / Practical Tips
→ If, after allocating all remaining electrons to terminal atoms, the central atom is short of an octet, don't panic — it means you need a multiple bond, not more total electrons.
→ Only convert a lone pair that belongs to an atom actually bonded to the electron-deficient central atom — you can't "borrow" electrons from an unrelated atom.
→ After forming the double bond, always re-verify both atoms: the donor (O) should still land at 8 (fewer lone pairs, but more shared electrons compensates).
→ Hydrogen is always a dead end for multiple bonds — it caps out at 2 electrons total, no lone pairs to give away.
→ Double bonds shorten and strengthen the bond compared to a single bond — useful to mention if asked about bond length/strength trends in an interview.
