XeF₂ — Linear Expanded Octet

The simplest expanded-octet case: two bonds, three lone pairs, all on xenon
Problem
XeF₂ is the cleanest example of an expanded octet: only 2 bonds around the central atom, yet Xe still ends up holding more than 8 electrons because the leftover electrons — after satisfying both fluorines — have nowhere else to go.
How It Works

Electron Bookkeeping

Xe: 8 v.e.  +  F×2: 14 v.e.
Total = 22 valence e⁻

2 single bonds (Xe–F ×2) → −4 → 18 left
2 F atoms × 6 e⁻ each → −12 → 6 left
6 leftover e⁻ → all go to Xe → 3 lone pairs on Xe

Final Count Check

F (each): 6 lone-pair e⁻ + 2 shared = 8 ✓
Xe: 6 lone-pair e⁻ + 4 shared (2 bonds) = 10
Xe exceeds the octet — allowed, period 5 element
F–Xe–F linear geometry; Xe carries 3 lone pairs (6 e⁻) plus 2 bonding pairs = 10 e⁻ total

Real-World Example Scenarios
Scenario — XeF₂ as an industrial oxidizer/etchant: Xenon difluoride is actually used in the semiconductor industry as a dry-etching agent for silicon (it reacts spontaneously with Si to release SiF₄ gas — tying back to the very first SiF₄ example in this series). Its reactivity and linear shape both trace back directly to this Lewis diagram: 2 bonding pairs + 3 lone pairs on Xe gives a linear electron geometry (VSEPR), which is why the F–Xe–F molecule is straight, not bent.
Interview / Practical Tips
→ Leftover electrons after terminal atoms are satisfied always go to the central atom as lone pairs — that's the mechanical trigger for "expanded octet," not a special rule you invoke.
→ Don't confuse "few bonds" with "small electron count" — XeF₂ has only 2 bonds but 10 electrons around Xe once lone pairs are counted.
→ Central-atom lone pairs matter for shape: 2 bonds + 3 lone pairs (5 electron domains) predicts trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry, but the 3 lone pairs occupy equatorial positions, leaving a linear molecular shape — a common VSEPR follow-up question.
