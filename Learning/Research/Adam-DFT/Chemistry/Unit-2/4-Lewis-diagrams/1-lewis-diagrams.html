Lewis Diagrams

Mapping how atoms share and hold valence electrons — worked through SiF₄
Problem
Given a molecular formula, you often need to know which atoms are bonded to which, how many bonds exist between them, and where the leftover (lone pair) electrons sit. A Lewis diagram answers all three in one picture — it's the starting point for predicting molecular geometry, polarity, and reactivity.
How It Works

Worked Example: SiF₄ (Silicon Tetrafluoride)

  1. Count total valence electrons. Add valence electrons of every atom (multiply by count of each). Add 1 e⁻ per negative charge, subtract 1 e⁻ per positive charge.
  2. Pick the central atom — least electronegative element, excluding H. Everything else becomes terminal.
  3. Draw single bonds from center to each terminal atom. Each bond = 2 electrons. Subtract these from the running total.
  4. Fill terminal atoms with lone pairs until each has an octet (H only needs 2). Subtract these from the running total.
  5. Dump any leftovers on the central atom. If the central atom still lacks an octet, convert lone pairs into double/triple bonds.
Electron Count
Si: 4 valence e⁻ (group 14, shell 3)
F: 7 valence e⁻ × 4 atoms = 28
Total = 4 + 28 = 32 valence e⁻
Electron Bookkeeping
32 → 4 bonds drawn (−8) → 24 left
24 → 4 F atoms get 6 e⁻ each (−24) → 0 left
Si already has an octet (4 bonds) → done
4 Si–F single bonds · each F holds 3 lone pairs (6 e⁻) · Si and every F satisfy the octet rule

Real-World Example Scenarios
Scenario 1 — Semiconductor etching: SiF₄ is a real byproduct gas in silicon wafer etching (plasma etching with fluorine chemistry in chip fabrication). Knowing it's a symmetric, fully-bonded, nonpolar molecule (like CF₄) explains why it's volatile and easily pumped away as exhaust gas.
Scenario 2 — CO₂ vs SiF₄ (double bonds vs single bonds): In CO₂, carbon can't reach an octet with single bonds alone (4 e⁻ from single bonds + lone pairs won't fill C's octet), so step 4 kicks in — a lone pair on each O becomes a second bond, forming O=C=O. This is the branch of the algorithm SiF₄ never needed.
Interview / Practical Tips
→ Always total valence electrons before drawing anything — it's your budget, and every subsequent step subtracts from it.
→ Central atom = least electronegative (H is always terminal, never central, even if it's technically "electronegative enough").
→ If electrons run out before every atom (except H) hits an octet, that's your signal to add multiple bonds — don't just leave atoms electron-deficient.
→ Quick octet check at the end: central atom electrons in bonds + terminal atoms' shared + lone pair electrons should each total 8 (2 for H).
→ Common trap: forgetting to add/subtract electrons for ionic charge on polyatomic ions (e.g., SO₄²⁻ needs +2 e⁻ added to the total).
