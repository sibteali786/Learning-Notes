Lewis Diagrams: Ions & Triple Bonds

Worked through CN⁻ (cyanide anion) — charge adjustment and pushing past a double bond
Problem
Two new wrinkles show up here that neutral molecules like SiF₄ and CH₂O didn't have: the species carries a charge (so the electron count itself changes), and one double bond still isn't enough — the central-atom check can force a second conversion, turning a double bond into a triple bond.
How It Works

Worked Example: CN⁻ (Cyanide Anion)

  1. Count total valence electrons — sum the neutral atoms' v.e., then add 1 e⁻ per negative charge (or subtract 1 e⁻ per positive charge).
  2. Draw a single bond between the two atoms — with only two atoms, neither is "more central," just connect them.
  3. Allocate remaining electrons, starting with the more electronegative atom, chasing octets.
  4. Check every atom. If one is short, convert a lone pair from the other into another bond — repeat until both hit 8.
  5. Enclose the whole diagram in brackets with the overall charge shown outside, top right.

Electron Bookkeeping
10 → 1 single bond C–N (−2) → 8 left
8 → N gets 3 lone pairs (−6) → 2 left
2 → C gets 1 lone pair (−2) → 0 left
Check: N = 8 ✓   C = only 4 ✗
The Fix — Twice
C at 4 e⁻, N at 8 e⁻ → 1 N lone pair → 2nd bond (double bond) → C at 6, N at 8 — still short → another N lone pair → 3rd bond (triple bond) → C = 8, N = 8 ✓
Step 3 — single bond, N full at 8, C stuck at 4
Final — C≡N triple bond, both at 8, whole ion bracketed with −1

Real-World Example Scenarios
Scenario 1 — Cyanide toxicity: The C≡N triple bond in cyanide is short, strong, and highly polar — this is exactly why the cyanide ion binds so tightly to the iron center in cytochrome c oxidase, blocking cellular respiration. The bond order you derive here directly explains its binding strength.
Scenario 2 — Isoelectronic species: CN⁻ has the same electron count (10 valence e⁻ arrangement, triple bond) as N₂ and CO — all three are described as "isoelectronic." Once you can build one Lewis diagram with a triple bond, you can predict the structure of the other two without redoing the math from scratch.
Interview / Practical Tips
→ For any ion: add 1 e⁻ per unit of negative charge, subtract 1 e⁻ per unit of positive charge — do this once, right after the neutral-atom sum, before anything else.
→ With only 2 atoms, "central atom" doesn't apply — just connect them and let the octet check tell you the bond order.
→ Don't assume one conversion is enough — re-check every atom after each lone-pair-to-bond conversion; CN⁻ needs two conversions to reach a triple bond.
→ Always close ions in brackets with the charge as a superscript outside — an unbracketed diagram with the electrons all placed correctly is still an incomplete answer for a charged species.
→ Formal charge is the deeper check behind "which atom should be central / how many bonds": for CN⁻ minimizing formal charges independently confirms the triple-bond structure.
