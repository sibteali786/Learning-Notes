Resonance — NO₃⁻

Nitrate anion: three valid Lewis structures, one real molecule
Problem
Following the usual algorithm on NO₃⁻ gives a structure with one N=O double bond and two N–O single bonds — implying one shorter, stronger bond and two longer, weaker ones. But real nitrate has three identical bonds, same length, same energy. The single-structure algorithm undersells reality — that gap is exactly what resonance explains.
How It Works

Worked Example: NO₃⁻

Electron Count
N: 5 v.e.
O×3: 6 × 3 = 18 v.e.
neutral total = 23
charge −1 → +1 e⁻ → 24 total
Bookkeeping
24 → 3 single bonds N–O (−6) → 18 left
18 → 3 O × 6 e⁻ each (−18) → 0 left
Check N: only 6 e⁻ — short of octet
The Fix — and Why It's Ambiguous Same move as CH₂O: steal one lone pair from an O and turn it into a second N–O bond so nitrogen reaches 8. But here's the catch — any of the three oxygens could donate that lone pair. Nothing in the electron count favors one O over another, so there are three equally valid Lewis structures, not one "correct" answer.

Your Questions, Answered

What is resonance, specifically, here?

Resonance is what you call it when a molecule needs more than one valid Lewis structure to represent it, because more than one placement of a double bond (or lone pair) satisfies the electron count and the octet rule equally well. For NO₃⁻, the "extra" bonding pair could legitimately go to the top O, the bottom-left O, or the bottom-right O — three structures, all with the same total energy, same validity, connected by ↔ (not ⇌ — that symbol is reserved for chemical equilibrium, which this is not).

What do we mean by "resonance hybrid"?

None of the three individual structures is what actually exists in nature — nitrate doesn't flip between them. The resonance hybrid is the real, single structure: each N–O bond is a blend, roughly 1⅓ bonds (average of two single bonds and one double bond across three equivalent positions), and the "extra" pair of electrons is delocalized — spread evenly across all three N–O bonds rather than sitting in one specific double bond. This is why experiments show three identical bond lengths, all between a single and double bond in length, instead of two long bonds and one short one.

Real-World Example Scenarios
Scenario 1 — Why nitrate is so stable (fertilizers, explosives): The delocalization in NO₃⁻ spreads out (and stabilizes) the negative charge across all three oxygens instead of concentrating it on one atom. This extra "resonance stabilization" is part of why nitrate salts (like ammonium nitrate) are common, stable fertilizer components — the anion itself resists further reaction more than a hypothetical single-structure version would.
Scenario 2 — Benzene, the classic resonance example: The same idea scales up: benzene (C₆H₆) is usually drawn with alternating single/double bonds, but experimentally all six C–C bonds are identical length. Chemists draw benzene with a circle inside the ring specifically to represent the resonance hybrid — the same visual idea as the dashed partial-bond nitrate diagram.
Interview / Practical Tips
→ Whenever converting a lone pair into a bond could equally be done at more than one equivalent terminal atom, that's your signal you're dealing with resonance — draw all valid structures, don't just pick one.
→ Never say a molecule "switches between" resonance structures — say the true structure is a hybrid/blend, with delocalized electrons. This phrasing distinction is a common interview trip-up.
→ Equal resonance structures (like NO₃⁻'s three, all with the same atoms, charges, and bond arrangement pattern) contribute equally to the hybrid — unequal ones (later topic) don't, and the more stable one contributes more.
→ Real experimental clue for resonance: if measured bond lengths in a molecule are identical when a single Lewis diagram predicts unequal ones, suspect resonance immediately.
→ Don't forget the anion bracket + charge on every resonance structure — it's a complete Lewis diagram requirement even mid-resonance.
