Formal Charge — HNO₂

A bookkeeping tool for picking which resonance structure matters most
Problem
When a molecule has multiple valid resonance structures (like nitrous acid, HNO₂), they're not always equally "good." Formal charge gives each atom, in each structure, a number that flags how far it deviates from its free, neutral electron count — the structure with formal charges closest to zero is the one that contributes most to the real resonance hybrid.
How It Works
FC = VE − [LPE + ½(BE)]
VE = valence e⁻ of the free neutral atom  ·  LPE = lone-pair e⁻ on that atom in the molecule  ·  BE = bonding (shared) e⁻ around that atom
Plain-language version: draw a circle around one atom. Count its lone-pair electrons in full, plus half of every bond touching it (each bond = 2 shared e⁻, so you take 1 per bond). That sum is "allocated" to the atom. FC = free-atom valence e⁻ − allocated e⁻.

Key Distinctions
IdeaWhat it is NOTWhat it actually is
Formal chargeThe real charge on that atomA bookkeeping number assuming perfectly equal electron sharing (ignores electronegativity)
Sum of formal chargesCan be anythingAlways equals the overall charge on the molecule/ion (0 for a neutral molecule)
"Best" resonance structureArbitrarily chosenThe one whose formal charges are closest to 0 overall (and negative FC on the more electronegative atom, if any charge must appear)

Sum FC = molecule's total charge — a good way to sanity-check your arithmetic on any structure, resonance or not.

Real-World Example Scenarios
Scenario 1 — Predicting reactive sites: A nonzero formal charge often marks where a molecule is more reactive or unstable — the +1 oxygen in HNO₂'s minor structure is electron-poor and more likely to be attacked by a nucleophile, which is exactly the kind of prediction formal charge is used for in reaction mechanism analysis.
Scenario 2 — Choosing between candidate structures generally: Formal charge is the standard first filter chemists use (before even getting to electronegativity-based refinements) whenever a molecule like SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻, or O₃ has more than one plausible skeleton — it quickly rules out structures that pile up charge where it doesn't belong.
Interview / Practical Tips
→ Fast mental shortcut: FC = (group number) − (lone pair e⁻) − (number of bonds) — same formula, easier to run in your head during an exam.
→ Always double-check: the formal charges in one structure must sum to the molecule's actual overall charge (0 here, since HNO₂ is neutral) — a fast way to catch an arithmetic slip.
→ Formal charge ≠ real/partial charge from electronegativity — that's a separate, complementary concept covered later; formal charge purely assumes equal sharing.
→ When ranking resonance structures: (1) minimize the number of atoms with nonzero FC, (2) if charge must exist, put the negative FC on the more electronegative atom.
→ Don't confuse "formal charge of the whole atom set" with "charge of the ion/molecule" — formal charge is calculated per atom, then summed, not read off the bracket.
