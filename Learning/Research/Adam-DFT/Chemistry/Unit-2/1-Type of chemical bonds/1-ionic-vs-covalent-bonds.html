Ionic vs Covalent vs Metallic Bonds

How atoms stop drifting alone and start sticking together — three strategies across one spectrum.

Problem

Why do atoms bond at all?

Isolated atoms aren't stable unless their outer (valence) shell is full. An atom with too few or too many valence electrons is "unhappy" — it wants to reach a stable octet (8 valence electrons, like the noble gases). Bonding is just two atoms solving each other's electron problem — either by giving one away or by sharing one.

How it works — Ionic Bond

Electron transfer, not sharing

Take Na (1 valence electron, desperate to lose it) and Cl (7 valence electrons, desperate to gain one). Na fully hands its electron to Cl.

Result: two full ions, each with a complete octet, held together purely by electrostatic (+/−) attraction. No electron is shared — it belongs entirely to Cl now.

How it works — Covalent Bond

Electron sharing, not transfer

Two oxygen atoms each have 6 valence electrons (need 2 more). Neither can steal from the other — instead, each contributes one electron per bond to a shared pair.

Result: both atoms effectively reach 8 valence electrons by counting the shared pair twice — once for each atom. No full charges form (in a symmetric bond like O=O).

Special case — polar covalent

In H₂O, O and H still share electrons — but O is far more electronegative, so the shared electrons spend more time near O. This creates partial charges (δ⁺ / δ⁻), not full ones. Still one bond type — covalent — just unevenly shared.

How it works — Metallic Bond

Electrons shared by everyone — the "sea of electrons"

Take a chunk of iron (Fe). Every atom donates its valence electron(s) into a shared, delocalized pool. The electrons aren't shared between just two atoms (like covalent) — they belong to the whole structure.

Positive metal ions (Fe⁺) sit in a lattice, attracted to the surrounding electron sea, which holds the structure together — like ions floating in "pudding" made of electrons.

Why metals behave this way
  • Conductive: free electrons carry charge easily
  • Malleable / ductile: ions can slide past each other, sea of electrons keeps holding them — doesn't shatter
  • Contrast: an ionic solid (like NaCl) is rigid and shatters under stress, since shifting the ion layout brings same-charges together and they repel
Real-world: bending a copper wire vs. snapping a salt crystal. Copper bends because the electron sea "flows" around the shifting ions. Salt cracks because ionic bonds are directional and rigid — no sea to cushion the shift.
Key Distinction — your question

"Both share electrons — so what's actually different?"

They don't both share. That's the misconception to drop:

Think of it as a spectrum of electron sharing scope: ionic → no sharing, full transfer, polar covalent → unequal sharing between 2 atoms, nonpolar covalent → equal sharing between 2 atoms, metallic → equal sharing among ALL atoms at once.

Real-World Examples
Table salt (NaCl): Ionic. This is why salt is a hard crystal with a high melting point (788°C) — you're breaking a lattice of full + and − charges, not just one bond.
Water (H₂O): Polar covalent. The δ+/δ− charges are exactly why water molecules stick to each other (hydrogen bonding), why water is a great solvent for salts, and why ice floats.
Oxygen gas (O₂) in the air you breathe: Nonpolar covalent (double bond) — no charge imbalance since both atoms are identical, so it's an equal split.
Copper wiring / an iron nail: Metallic. The free-flowing electron sea is exactly why metals conduct electricity and can be hammered/bent into shape (malleable, ductile) instead of shattering.
Practical / Exam Tips
  • Quick test: metal + nonmetal → almost always ionic. Nonmetal + nonmetal → covalent.
  • If asked "is this bond polar?" — check electronegativity difference: 0 = nonpolar covalent, small–medium = polar covalent, large = ionic (rough cutoffs: <0.5 nonpolar, 0.5–1.7 polar, >1.7 ionic).
  • Don't say "ionic bonds share electrons" on an exam — that's the #1 trap. Ionic = transfer + attraction. Covalent = share (2 atoms). Metallic = share (all atoms, delocalized).
  • Metals conduct electricity in solid AND liquid form (free electrons); ionic compounds only conduct when molten/dissolved (ions must be mobile). This is a classic exam differentiator.
  • Lewis dot structures: a shared pair between atoms = one line = one covalent bond. A dot next to only one atom with a charge (⁺/⁻) = ionic, not shared.
Memory hook: "I" in Ionic = "I get it all" (transfer). "Co" in Covalent = "Co-own it" (2-atom share). Metallic = "Melting-pot" (everyone shares).
