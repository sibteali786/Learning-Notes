Metals & Alloys

Metallic bonding, the "sea of electrons," and why mixing atoms changes a metal's properties

Problem Statement

Pure metals are shiny, malleable, and conduct electricity — properties that come from how metal atoms bond. An alloy is a mixture of elements (usually mostly one metal + something else) that keeps these metallic properties, but often improves them.

The question this covers: how do you mix a second element into a metal, and does the way you mix it (based on atom size) change the kind of alloy you get?

How It Works

Metallic bonding: metal atoms aren't very electronegative, so they don't hoard their valence electrons. Instead, every atom contributes its valence electrons to a shared, delocalized "sea of electrons" — leaving behind a lattice of positive metal ions floating in that sea.

Sea of electrons model
Positive metal ions (blue) fixed in a lattice; delocalized valence electrons (green) free to move — this mobility is why metals conduct electricity and can bend (malleable) without breaking bonds.
💡 Charge on the ions: Group 1 metals contribute 1 electron each → +1 ions. Group 2 metals or transition metals (like Fe) can contribute more → +2 or +3 ions in the sea.

Alloys are formed by mixing a second element into this metal lattice. What determines how it fits in is the relative atom size of the two elements.

Key Distinctions — Alloy Types

Interstitial
Steel (Fe + C). Small atoms (carbon) squeeze into the gaps between much larger atoms (iron).
Substitutional
Brass (Cu + Zn). Similar-sized atoms — some copper positions are directly swapped out for zinc in the same lattice sites.
Both
Stainless steel (Fe + Cr + C). Similar-sized Cr substitutes for Fe; small carbon still fits interstitially — both mechanisms at once.

Real-World Example Scenario

🧪 Scenario: The ISS pressurized modules and your kitchen cutlery both rely on stainless steel specifically because the added chromium forms a thin, self-healing oxide layer that resists corrosion — critical for spacecraft exposed to extreme conditions, and for kitchenware exposed to moisture and acids. Meanwhile plain carbon steel (interstitial only) is much stronger than pure iron because the interstitial carbon atoms disrupt the regular iron lattice, making it harder for layers of atoms to slide past each other — which is why blades hold an edge better than pure iron would.

Practical / Interview Tips

  • Quick test: compare atomic radii of the two elements. Big size mismatch → interstitial. Similar size → substitutional. Both present → both mechanisms.
  • Carbon's small size (2nd shell valence electrons) vs. iron's larger size (4th shell valence electrons) is the textbook interstitial example — remember it as your reference point.
  • Copper and zinc are both similarly sized transition/post-transition metals — the textbook substitutional example (brass).
  • "Why does alloying change properties?" → disrupting the regular lattice (via size mismatch or substitution) makes it harder for atom layers to slide past each other, which is why alloys are often stronger/harder than the pure base metal.
