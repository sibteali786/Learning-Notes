Orbitals & Electron Probability

AP Chemistry · Atomic Structure · Source: Khan Academy (video)

Problem Why not just say electrons orbit like planets?

Early models (protons/neutrons in the center, electrons circling like planets) were intuitive — opposite charges attract, so electrons stay near the positive nucleus. But this turned out to be wrong. Electrons don't travel in fixed circular/elliptical paths. At any instant you can't know exactly where one is — you can only describe the probability of finding it in a given region.
Key shift in thinking
Replace "electron orbit" (a path) with "electron orbital" (a 3D probability cloud — the region where the electron is found ~90% of the time).

Model The Hydrogen Atom — Simplest Case

Orbital math is best worked out for hydrogen because it's the simplest atom: one proton, no neutrons (in the common isotope), one electron. The lone electron isn't literally circling the proton — it's "smeared" across a probability cloud. The sphere drawn is just the boundary containing 90% of that probability.

Concept Adding Energy = Quantum Jumps

With a planet or satellite, adding energy grows the orbit gradually. Electrons don't work that way — quantum means discrete packets, not "small." An electron doesn't gradually drift outward; it jumps to the next energy level once given exactly enough energy.
Energy addedResultShape
None (ground state)Stays in 1sSphere, close to nucleus
Enough to reach 2nd shellJumps to 2sLarger sphere
More, within 2nd shellJumps to 2pDumbbell
Even more3rd shell, d orbitals, etc.Increasingly exotic shapes
Why dumbbells and weird shapes?
Electrons have wave-like properties, similar to standing waves on a shaken rope or a drum membrane. Just as a vibrating membrane forms nodes and peaks, higher-energy electron "waves" form more complex 3D standing-wave patterns — that's where p, d, f shapes come from.

Terminology Shell vs Subshell vs Orbital

These three words are often confused — here's the hierarchy:
TermWhat it meansExample
Shell (= energy level)A whole energy tier1st shell, 2nd shell
Subshell (= sublevel)A type within a shells, p, d, f
OrbitalA specific region holding ≤2 electrons2px, 2py, 2pz
Shell 1 is a special case
The first shell has only ONE subshell (1s), which has only ONE orbital. So for shell 1, "shell," "subshell," and "orbital" all refer to the same thing. Starting at shell 2, they diverge: shell 2 has 2s (1 orbital) AND 2p (3 orbitals: 2px, 2py, 2pz).

Reference Filling Order (Hydrogen Example)

  • 1s holds up to 2 electrons (fills first — lowest energy)
  • Once 1s is full, the 3rd electron goes to 2s
  • 2s holds up to 2 more → 4 electrons total between 1s + 2s
  • The 5th electron must go into one of the three 2p orbitals
Electrons repel each other — that's why a 2nd electron won't crowd into an already-occupied orbital until lower-energy spots are full.

Key Distinctions

Old (planetary) modelOrbital (quantum) model
Electron follows a fixed pathElectron location is probabilistic
Energy added → orbit grows smoothlyEnergy added → discrete jump to new level
All orbits are circles/ellipsesOrbital shapes vary: sphere, dumbbell, more complex

Practical Tips

  • "Orbital" = probability region, NOT a path. Don't picture electrons as tiny planets.
  • s orbitals are always spherical, at every shell — they just get bigger at higher shells (1s vs 2s).
  • Each orbital holds a max of 2 electrons — this is true regardless of shape.
  • Shell = energy level = row-ish concept; subshell = shape type (s,p,d,f); orbital = the individual "slot."
  • Remember the numbering trick: shell number appears before the letter (2p = p subshell of the 2nd shell).
