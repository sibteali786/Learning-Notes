Identifying a Contaminant via % Composition

Worked example — mystery salt sample, 73% Cl by mass

Problem What Are We Solving?

A solid is known to be mostly NaCl, possibly contaminated with NaI, KCl, or LiCl. Analysis shows the sample is 73% chlorine by mass — well above pure NaCl. The task: figure out which contaminant (if any) could explain that jump, using only % Cl by mass.

Core Strategy: Compare % Cl of Each Candidate

% Cl by mass = molar mass of Cl / molar mass of whole compound

If pure NaCl's % Cl is below 73%, mixing in a compound with an even lower % Cl (or 0%) can only pull the average down — never up. Only a contaminant with a higher % Cl than pure NaCl could explain the jump to 73%.

Result

The sample is not pure NaCl. Since NaI and KCl both have lower (or zero) % Cl than NaCl, they're ruled out. Only LiCl (≈84% Cl) has a high enough chlorine content to raise the mixture's average from ~61% up to the observed 73%. The sample is likely a mix of NaCl + LiCl.

Shortcut No-Calculation Reasoning

  • Same numerator (Cl's molar mass, 35.45) across every candidate compound.
  • Bigger denominator → lower % Cl (K has a bigger molar mass than Na → KCl % Cl < NaCl % Cl).
  • Smaller denominator → higher % Cl (Li is much lighter than Na → LiCl % Cl > NaCl % Cl).
  • This lets you rank candidates by comparing molar masses alone — no full division needed until you've narrowed it down.

Tips Practical Tips

Tip When comparing % composition of a series of similar compounds (same anion, different cation), a smaller cation molar mass always means a higher % of the shared anion by mass — no need to calculate all of them fully.
Tip A contaminant with 0% of the element in question (like NaI has 0% Cl) always pulls the mixture's average down, so it can be ruled out immediately if the observed % is higher than the pure compound's %.
Tip Once you know the two pure % values (e.g. 61% and 84%) and the mixture's actual % (73%), you can solve for the exact mixing ratio using a weighted-average (mixture) equation — same math as the isotope abundance problems.
