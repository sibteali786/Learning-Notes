📝 Thinking on Paper

The 3-principle system that cut learning time in half

Problem Statement

As you consume more new information, confusion and overwhelm rise with it. The instinctive fix — write it all down — feels productive but usually isn't: if you obsess over making notes correct, tidy, and complete, you get paralyzed by perfectionism and bypass the actual cognitive work (organizing, connecting, correcting) that creates real understanding. It's the mental equivalent of getting tired on a run and just driving the rest of the way.

How It Works — 3 Principles

1

Make it wrong

Dump keywords on the page fast, guess at how they connect or group. Accuracy doesn't matter yet — you're laying puzzle pieces on the table, not solving the puzzle in your head. Perfectionism here is the enemy.

2

Make it shorter

No full sentences — keywords only, no need for neat handwriting. Retention drops as word count rises: distilling an idea into one word forces more processing than writing a paragraph, and short notes let your brain scan for patterns faster.

3

Make it again

When the page gets messy or you spot wrong connections, reorganize: regroup, rearrange, reconnect. This is where most of the actual learning happens — the act of fixing your own mistakes is what consolidates memory.

Key Distinctions

❌ Regular note-taking

  • Full sentences, trying to be accurate first try
  • Obsessing over neatness/organization upfront
  • Notes pile up, rarely revisited or restructured
  • Feels productive, but is often a "mental trash bin"

✅ Thinking on paper

  • Keywords only, guesses allowed, messy first draft
  • Perfection deliberately deferred
  • Actively reorganized once it gets messy or wrong
  • The reorganizing itself is the learning mechanism

Real-World Example

Scenario: Learning memory-related terms from a cognitive neuroscience book for the first time.
Step 1 — Make it wrong: Scatter keywords from ~1.5 pages of reading: encoding, retrieval, memory, cognitive load, thalamus, explicit memory, working memory, long-term memory. Rough first guess: "encoding + retrieval relate to memory," "explicit/working/long-term memory" grouped loosely under "types."
Step 2 — Make it shorter:
"Write down key words quickly" → fast "Look at what you've written" → examine "Make some guesses at connections" → guess / connect
Step 3 — Make it again: After more reading, the "types" grouping turns out wrong — explicit memory actually overlaps with working and long-term memory, they aren't cleanly separate. Regroup, rearrange, reconnect. That correction is where the real learning happens, not the initial read.

Interview / Practical Tips

💡 Perfectionism check: If you notice yourself avoiding writing something because it might be "wrong," that's the exact moment to write it anyway.
💡 Word-count test: If a note is a full sentence, compress it to a keyword — harder to do, but forces deeper processing.
💡 Messy = signal: When your notes/mind-map start feeling cluttered or you spot a mistake, that's the trigger to reorganize — not a sign you did it wrong.
💡 Applies broadly: Works in meetings, live problem-solving, or long study sessions — not just formal note-taking.
