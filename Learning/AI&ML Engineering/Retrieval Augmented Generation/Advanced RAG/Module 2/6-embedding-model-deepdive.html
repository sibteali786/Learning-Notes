How embedding models learn meaning

An embedding model must put similar text at nearby vectors and dissimilar text far apart. It learns this by being pushed and pulled, over and over, using pairs of texts that are labelled similar or dissimilar.

The job, in terms of pairs

How could a computer possibly understand the meaning of a piece of text? The model never “understands” directly. It’s judged on whether it places pairs correctly.

Positive pair

Two pieces of similar text. They should be embedded close together.

Two pieces of dissimilar meaning. They should be embedded farther apart.

Training, step by step

1
Compile a huge set of positive and negative pairs (“examples”).

Often multi-millions of pairs. Each word or text appears in many examples, to capture its relationship to a wide variety of texts and concepts.

2
Start from random vectors.

At the beginning, every text is embedded to a random vector: nonsense with no relation to meaning. Used for retrieval, an untrained model gives gibberish.

3
Score the placements.

The model checks all the pairs: how well did I place positives together and negatives apart? Because it uses the contrast between positive and negative examples to evaluate itself, this is called contrastive training.

4
Update internal parameters.

An algorithm adjusts the parameters to move positive pairs closer and negative pairs farther apart.

5
Repeat many times.

Re-embed with the updated parameters, evaluate again, update again. After many rounds positives are pulled close and negatives pushed far apart.

At real scale it gets messy. You’d never train on just two pairs. With millions of anchors, positives and negatives, every vector is pushed and pulled in many directions at once. That’s why models use hundreds or thousands of dimensions: more room to satisfy nuanced relationships.

Why you never mix vectors from different models

Each model has different training data, a different number of dimensions, and different random starting values. Comparing vectors from two models is nonsense. Here’s a toy proof: train “Model A” in the lab above, then train a second model on the same data from a different random start.

Train Model A in the lab first (50+ rounds), then press the button.

Practical consequence (my addition): if you ever switch embedding models, you must re-embed your whole knowledge base with the new model, and embed prompts with that same model, or the distances are meaningless.

Key takeaways from the lesson

Abstract and somewhat random

Semantic vectors don’t have human-readable axes. Before training, locations in space have no meaning.

Meaning comes from clusters

After training, a location is meaningful only because a cluster of similar concepts formed there (a lion cluster, a trombone cluster).

Only compare within one model

Different training data, dimensions and random initial values make cross-model comparisons nonsense.

In practice: you don’t need to train an embedding model to build a RAG system. You’ll likely use off-the-shelf models, which do a remarkably good job placing similar words, sentences or documents together, and you probably won’t implement the distance measures yourself. Understanding the training just helps you reason about how to use them.

The toy trainer in plain Python

The same push and pull used in the lab, for intuition only. Real models adjust neural-network parameters, not the vectors directly.

import math, random

def train_round(vecs, positives, negatives, lr=0.08, margin=5.0):
    move = [[0.0, 0.0] for _ in vecs]
    for i, j in positives:                      # pull similar pairs together
        for k in range(2):
            d = vecs[j][k] - vecs[i][k]
            move[i][k] += lr * d
            move[j][k] -= lr * d
    for i, j in negatives:                      # push dissimilar pairs apart
        dx, dy = vecs[j][0] - vecs[i][0], vecs[j][1] - vecs[i][1]
        dist = math.hypot(dx, dy) or 1e-6
        if dist < margin:
            push = (margin - dist) * lr * 0.5
            move[i][0] -= dx / dist * push; move[i][1] -= dy / dist * push
            move[j][0] += dx / dist * push; move[j][1] += dy / dist * push
    for v, m in zip(vecs, move):
        v[0] += m[0]; v[1] += m[1]

vecs = [[random.uniform(-5, 5), random.uniform(-5, 5)] for _ in range(9)]  # random start
for _ in range(100):
    train_round(vecs, positives, negatives)

For real projects: use an existing embedding model rather than training one. If you do want to fine-tune on your own pairs, check the current documentation of the library you choose, since training APIs change quickly. I haven’t verified any specific library’s current API here.

Alternatives and trade-offs

The lesson’s advice is to use off-the-shelf models. Here’s how the options compare (the last two go beyond the lesson).

Off-the-shelf embedding model

Pros
  • No training data or compute needed
  • Works remarkably well for general text
Cons
  • May miss niche jargon in your domain

Fine-tune on your own pairs

Pros
  • Adapts to domain vocabulary (for example your product terms)
Cons
  • Needs good positive and negative pairs and evaluation; must re-embed everything afterwards

Train from scratch

Pros
  • Full control over data and dimensions
Cons
  • Multi-million example datasets and serious compute: rarely worth it for a RAG system

Hybrid search around it

Pros
  • Keyword search covers exact terms the model blurs
Cons
  • More components to tune (next lessons)

Summary in six lines

  • An embedding model’s job: similar text to nearby vectors, dissimilar text to distant vectors.
  • It learns from massive collections of positive and negative pairs.
  • Training starts from random vectors, then loops: embed, score with the pairs, update parameters. This is contrastive training.
  • Each vector is pushed and pulled by many pairs at once, which is why models use hundreds or thousands of dimensions.
  • Afterwards, locations have meaning only because clusters of similar concepts formed there; rerunning training puts the same clusters in different places.
  • Only compare vectors from the same embedding model. Next: using these dense vectors in your retriever.
