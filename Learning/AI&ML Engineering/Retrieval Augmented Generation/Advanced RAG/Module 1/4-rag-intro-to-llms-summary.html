🧠 RAG: Introduction to LLMs

DeepLearning.AI · Retrieval Augmented Generation · Module 1 · "Introduction to LLMs" (Zain Hasan)

Why Start With the LLM

Now that you've seen the high-level architecture of a RAG system, this lecture looks at each component, starting with the LLM itself. Understanding how LLMs operate, their strengths and limitations, explains why the other components in a RAG system are designed the way they are, to improve an LLM's performance.

1. LLMs = "Fancy Autocomplete"

Jokingly called fancy autocomplete, but a fair description: all LLMs do is predict the next word that should appear in a piece of text.

Prompt

What a beautiful day, the sun is  

Completions

What a beautiful day the sun is shining

What a beautiful day the sun is rising

What a beautiful day the sun is out

Probable

"Shining" makes the most sense; "rising" or "out" could also work.

Improbable ("the sun is exploding")

Not a grammar problem: it's valid English. It's incorrect only because the phrase is improbable. The sun usually doesn't explode, certainly not on a beautiful day.

You have an intuitive sense for how words are used, and in a way, so do language models.

2. Under the Hood: A Neural Network

An enormous and complex mathematical model of language. It stores:

  • which words are commonly used with each other (slide: which words frequently appear together)
  • in which order they typically appear
  • on a higher level, what these words mean in context (contextual meaning)
This mathematical representation of language is what the model ultimately uses to generate new text. (Slide shows "sun" → "shining".)

3. Generating Text: One Token at a Time

When an LLM generates a completion, it just adds new words to the end of the prompt, one at a time. For the running example it would likely add shining, then inthesky one at a time, before signaling the completion is done and returning it to the user.

Technically an LLM doesn't generate words but tokens, a more generic term for pieces of words.

Token (slide)

  • a piece of a word
  • some words get single tokens
  • compound words use multiple tokens
  • punctuation marks
  • ~10,000 – 100,000 tokens in an LLM's vocabulary, allowing models to represent any possible word with fewer tokens
Slide examples

Single tokens: London door

Compound (multiple tokens): unhappy   programmatically

Punctuation: Completely, I agree!

Lecture: period, comma and question mark could also get their own tokens. Most LLMs have a total vocabulary of anywhere from 10 to more than 100,000 tokens (as stated in the lecture audio). Building compound words out of smaller word pieces lets the model build any possible word without assigning a token to each one.

4. The 3-Step Loop Before Every New Token

① Process current state

Generate a deep understanding of the relationships between each word and the overall meaning of the text.

② Calculate probabilities

Go through every token in the vocabulary (usually tens to hundreds of thousands) and calculate the probability it should appear next.

③ Select next token

Randomly choose the next token from that probability distribution.

Round 1 — prompt: "What a beautiful day, the sun is" (vocabulary ~10,000 – 100,000 tokens)

Selected: shining — 80 times out of a hundred it'll choose "shining", but it's still possible it could choose "rising" or, yes, even "exploding". Even improbable words like exploding or snoring have a tiny chance.

Round 2 — the loop repeats with "shining" now part of the text ("new tokens make sense in context of old ones"):

Selected: in (only 10%, but chosen). When the LLM adds another token, it repeats this entire process. Most words are from the original prompt, but one of them is "shining", which it just generated. So token choices made earlier impact selections made later. This is desirable: new tokens make sense in the context of tokens already chosen.

5. Autoregressive = "Self-Influencing"

Once an LLM randomly chooses one direction, it follows that path where it leads.

  • "Self-influencing"
  • New tokens make sense in context of old ones
  • Running the same prompt leads to different completions
Lecture: after choosing "shining", the LLM might choose "in", "the", "sky" since those make sense given what came before. If the first generated token had been "warming", it might then choose "our faces". Thanks to the randomness and the autoregressive nature, running the same prompt multiple times through the same LLM will usually lead to different completions.

6. How LLMs Learn (Training)

An LLM understands prompts and makes reasonable predictions because it was trained on large collections of text. The model has billions of individual parameters (numerical weights).

StageWhat happens
Before trainingThe model would just produce gibberish.
During trainingThe LLM is shown incomplete pieces of text from the training data and tries to predict which word comes next.
UpdateBased on the accuracy of its predictions, it updates its internal parameters.
ResultThe model learns to produce both the factual information and the linguistic styles in the training data.
Many large language models today are trained on trillions of tokens of text, largely from the open internet. The resulting models can generate text in a wide variety of styles on a wide variety of topics, because examples of those styles and information on those topics were in the training data. Understanding how LLMs work and are trained also explains many of their behaviors.

7. Why LLMs Hallucinate

Generate probable word sequences

LLMs just reproduce statistical patterns from their training data.

Knowledge gaps cause inaccurate responses

Responses can "sound right" but aren't true.

Truthful ≠ probable

LLMs are designed to generate "probable" text, not truthful text.

Lecture: if you ask an LLM about your company's private internal data or today's news, the model almost certainly wasn't trained on it, so it isn't in a good position to respond. Sometimes it gives responses that sound right but aren't true. Even though these are called hallucinations, the LLM isn't having a psychological episode or even really malfunctioning. Truth, as far as an LLM is concerned, is just that a sequence of words is probabilistically likely based on its training data.

8. How RAG Solves the Problem

With high-quality training data, our intuitive understanding of truth and an LLM's mathematical understanding of probable word sequences can be kept in alignment. The challenge is to ensure the LLM has access to as much relevant information as possible.

RAG takes advantage of how well LLMs understand context. If your RAG system adds relevant information to the prompt, the LLM can understand and incorporate it into its responses, even though that information wasn't part of its training data. Typically you'd say this information grounds the LLM's responses.

9. Why Not Add Everything to the Prompt?

You might want to add as much relevant information as you can find. In practice, you can't:

① Higher computational cost
  • Longer prompts take more computation to run
  • Before generating each new token, the model performs a computationally complex scan of every token already in the completion, including the original prompt
  • The scan happens before generating each new token
② Context window limit
  • Eventually you hit the limit of the LLM's context window: the longest sequence it can process at once
  • Smaller/older models: only a few thousand tokens
  • Largest/newer models: millions
As the retriever adds more information to the augmented prompt, at first you'll just make prompts more costly, and eventually you'll entirely use up the model's context window.

Interview / Practical Tips

  • One-liner: an LLM is a neural network that predicts the next token from a probability distribution over its whole vocabulary.
  • Know tokens vs words: tokens are pieces of words; compound words split into multiple tokens; punctuation gets tokens too.
  • The per-token loop: process state → calculate probabilities → select (randomly) next token.
  • "Autoregressive" = self-influencing. Randomness + autoregression is why the same prompt gives different completions.
  • Hallucination in one line: LLMs generate probable text, not truthful text; knowledge gaps make it "sound right but not be true".
  • Explain why RAG can't just stuff everything in the prompt: computational cost (scan of every token before each new token) and the context window limit.
  • RAG works because LLMs understand context: retrieved info in the prompt grounds the response without retraining.
