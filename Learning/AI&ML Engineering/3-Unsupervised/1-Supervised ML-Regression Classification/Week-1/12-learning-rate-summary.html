The Learning Rate α: Too Small, Too Large, and Just Right DeepLearning.AI notes

Goal: Understand how the choice of α affects gradient descent — and why it can automatically slow down near a minimum even with a fixed α.
w := w − α · (d/dw) J(w)

1. α Too Small

What happens: multiplying the derivative by a tiny α (e.g. 0.0000001) produces tiny update steps. Gradient descent still moves toward the minimum — correctly — but extremely slowly, needing a huge number of iterations.

Verdict: works, but wastes time/compute.

2. α Too Large

What happens: even starting close to the minimum, a large α causes a huge step that overshoots past the minimum to the other side — increasing J instead of decreasing it. The next step overshoots again, even further away. Each iteration gets worse, not better.

Verdict: gradient descent may fail to converge, or actively diverge (cost keeps growing instead of shrinking).

3. What Happens Exactly At a Minimum?

At a local minimum, the tangent line is flat → slope = 0 → derivative = 0.
w := w − α · 0  =  w
So the update leaves w completely unchanged. Example: if w = 5 and α = 0.1, after one iteration w is still 5 — gradient descent naturally "locks in" once it finds a minimum, without needing any special stopping rule.

4. Why Fixed α Still Slows Down Automatically

  • Far from the minimum → curve is steep → derivative is large → big update step
  • Getting closer → curve flattens → derivative shrinks → smaller update step
  • Very close to the minimum → derivative nearly 0 → tiny update step
  • At the minimum → derivative = 0 → no further change
Even though α itself never changes, the step size shrinks automatically as you approach the minimum, because the derivative (slope) naturally decreases. This is why you don't need to manually shrink α over time for gradient descent to settle at a minimum.

Real-World Framing

Example: Think of parking a car by pressing the gas pedal. Too light a tap (small α) and you creep toward the parking spot forever. Too hard a stomp (large α) and you overshoot past the spot, have to reverse, overshoot again — swinging back and forth or crashing further away each time. A good driver eases off the gas automatically as they get close — which is exactly what gradient descent does on its own as the slope flattens near the minimum.
