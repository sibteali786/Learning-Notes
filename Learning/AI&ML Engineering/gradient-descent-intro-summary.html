Gradient Descent: Finding the Minimum of J DeepLearning.AI notes

Goal: Introduce gradient descent — a systematic algorithm to find the w, b that minimize J(w,b), instead of guessing values by hand. This is one of the most important building blocks in ML, used far beyond linear regression, including deep learning.

1. Generalizing Beyond Linear Regression

minimize J(w1, w2, …, wn, b)

Gradient descent works for any cost function, not just the squared-error cost for linear regression — including models with many parameters (w1…wn), and cost functions used in neural networks.

2. The Hill-Descending Analogy

Picture standing on a hilly landscape where the height = cost J(w,b), and your position (w, b) = your location on the hill. Your goal: reach the bottom of a valley (minimum cost) as efficiently as possible.
  • Start somewhere: pick initial values for w and b (common default for linear regression: w=0, b=0)
  • Look around 360°: figure out which direction takes you downhill fastest — this is the direction of steepest descent
  • Take a tiny step in that direction — this updates w and b slightly
  • Repeat: from the new position, look around again and take another step
  • Stop when you reach the bottom of a valley — a local minimum, where J is as small as possible in that region

3. The Local Minima Property

Important quirk: for complex (non-bowl-shaped) cost functions — like those from neural networks — there can be more than one valley (local minimum). Squared-error cost for linear regression is always bowl/hammock-shaped, so it has only one minimum — but general cost functions may not be.

Once you're descending into one valley, gradient descent won't "hop over" the hill to reach the other valley — the starting point can determine which minimum you land in.

Real-World Framing

Example: Imagine hiking down a foggy mountain with only a flashlight — you can't see the whole landscape, only the slope right under your feet. At each spot, you check which direction slopes downward most steeply and take a small step that way. Repeat until the ground stops sloping down. That's gradient descent: it doesn't need to "see" the entire cost surface, just the local slope at each step.
