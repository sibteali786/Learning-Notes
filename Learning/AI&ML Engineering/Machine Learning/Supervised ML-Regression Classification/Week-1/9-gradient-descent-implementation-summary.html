Implementing Gradient Descent DeepLearning.AI notes

Goal: Turn the "walk downhill" intuition from the last video into the actual update equations for w and b — and learn the one implementation detail (simultaneous update) that's easy to get wrong.

1. The Update Rule

w := w − α · (d/dw) J(w,b)
b := b − α · (d/db) J(w,b)

:= here is assignment (like w = w - ... in code), not a mathematical truth claim. In math notation "=" can mean either; in code, assignment is = and equality-testing is ==.

2. The Two Pieces of the Update

TermNameRole
α (alpha)Learning rateA small positive number (0–1, e.g. 0.01) that controls step size. Large α → aggressive/big steps; small α → cautious/tiny steps.
d/dw J(w,b)Derivative termComes from calculus — tells you the direction to step (combined with α, also affects step size). No calculus background needed; intuition comes next video.
You do not need to know calculus to use or understand gradient descent in this course — the derivative term's meaning will be built up intuitively in the next video.

3. Repeat Until Convergence

Repeat both update steps (for w and b) until the algorithm converges — i.e., w and b stop changing much with each additional step. This mirrors taking repeated baby steps downhill until you reach the valley floor.

4. The Critical Detail: Simultaneous Update

Both parameters must be updated at the same time, using the old values of w and b in both computations.

✅ Correct (simultaneous) 
tempW = w - α * d/dw J(w,b)
tempB = b - α * d/db J(w,b)
w = tempW
b = tempB
Both right-hand sides are computed first, using the original w and b, before either variable is overwritten.
❌ Incorrect (sequential) 
tempW = w - α * d/dw J(w,b)
w = tempW              <- updated too early!
tempB = b - α * d/db J(w,b)  <- uses NEW w
b = tempB
Here, the second line's derivative term uses the already-updated w, silently changing the math. This is a different (and not standard) algorithm.
Rule of thumb: whenever someone says "gradient descent," they mean the simultaneous update version. The non-simultaneous version may still sort of work in practice, but it's technically a different algorithm — always implement the correct simultaneous form.

Real-World Framing

Example: Think of adjusting two oven dials (temperature and time) to bake the perfect cake. You shouldn't change the temperature dial, taste-test, then decide how much to change the time dial based on that new temperature — you should decide both adjustments based on the current state of the cake at once, then apply both changes together. That's simultaneous update.
