Lesson 7 — Establishing a Baseline Level of Performance

What "high" or "low" actually means for J_train and J_cv — you can't judge them in a vacuum.

1 The Speech Recognition Puzzle

Training a speech recognition system on web-search audio clips gives:

Human level performance10.6%
↕ 0.2% gap
Training error (J_train)10.8%
↕ 4.0% gap
Cross-validation error (J_cv)14.8%
The trap

At first glance, 10.8% training error looks bad on its own — "10% wrong" sounds high. You might conclude high bias. But even human transcribers get 10.6% wrong on this data — a lot of web-search audio is genuinely noisy ("I'm going to navigate to [inaudible]"), so no algorithm, however good, can realistically beat that. 10.8% is actually excellent — just 0.2% off human performance.

The real story is in the second gap: J_cv (14.8%) is 4% higher than J_train (10.8%). That gap is what signals the actual problem.

Diagnosis: High Variance — not high bias

2 Why You Need a Baseline

Raw error numbers are meaningless without something to compare them to. A baseline level of performance is the answer to: "what error rate can I realistically hope to reach?" For inherently noisy or ambiguous data (audio, images, handwriting), that number is rarely 0%.

Human-level performance — best benchmark for unstructured data (audio, images, text), since humans are naturally excellent at these tasks.
Competing algorithm's performance — a prior implementation, an existing product, or a competitor's system.
Guess based on prior experience — informed estimate when neither of the above is available.

3 The Two Gaps That Actually Matter

Gap 1 — Baseline vs. J_train

Large gap → high bias. Your model isn't even reaching what's realistically achievable on this data.

Gap 2 — J_train vs. J_cv

Large gap → high variance. Your model does much better on data it has seen than data it hasn't.

4 Four Patterns You'll See in Practice

Baseline10.6%
J_train10.8%
J_cv14.8%
gap1 = 0.2% · gap2 = 4.0%

Baseline10.6%
J_train15.0%

gap1 = 4.4% · gap2 = 0.5%
High Bias
Baseline10.6%
J_train15.0%
J_cv19.7%
gap1 = 4.4% · gap2 = 4.7%
High Bias + High Variance

Same underlying numbers, three completely different diagnoses — purely based on where the gaps sit, not the raw error values.

5 Key Takeaway

Don't ask "is my training error large?" in isolation — ask "is my training error large relative to what I can reasonably hope to achieve?" Sometimes that achievable floor is 0% (clean, unambiguous data); often, especially for messy real-world audio/image/text data, it isn't. Establishing this baseline turns two raw percentages into an actual diagnosis of bias vs. variance vs. both.
