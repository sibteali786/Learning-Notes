Lesson 19 — Precision/Recall Tradeoff and F1 Score

Precision and recall usually pull against each other — the threshold decides where you land, and F1 score gives one number to compare algorithms.

1 The Threshold Controls the Tradeoff

Logistic regression outputs a probability 0 < f(x) < 1. The usual rule predicts 1 if f(x) ≥ 0.5. But that 0.5 cutoff is just a default — you can move it, and moving it changes precision and recall in opposite directions.

Raise the threshold → High Precision, Lower Recall

"Only diagnose if very confident" — appropriate when treatment is invasive/expensive and mild consequences don't demand aggressive treatment.

predict 1 if f(x) ≥ 0.7 or even 0.9
Lower the threshold → Lower Precision, Higher Recall

"When in doubt, predict positive" — appropriate when missing a real case is far worse than a false alarm, and treatment isn't too costly or invasive.

predict 1 if f(x) ≥ 0.3

2 The Precision-Recall Curve

Sweeping the threshold from 0.99 down to 0.01 traces this curve — high threshold sits top-left (high precision, low recall), low threshold sits bottom-right (low precision, high recall).

Threshold selection is a judgment call specific to the application's real-world costs — this is not something cross-validation picks for you automatically. You plot the curve, then manually choose the point that reflects the right tradeoff between false positives and false negatives for your use case.

The average is fooled by the same trick as accuracy

Algorithm 3 (precision 0.02, recall 1.0) has the highest average — but this is essentially a model that predicts positive for almost everyone (like print("y=1") always). It "catches" every real case only because it flags nearly everyone as positive, making it practically useless despite its perfect recall. A plain average rewards this degenerate strategy.

5 The F1 Score

F1 is the standard way to combine precision and recall into one number — it gives more weight to whichever of the two is lower, so an algorithm can't hide a terrible precision (or recall) behind a great score on the other.

F1 = 1 / (½·(1/P + 1/R)) = 2PR / (P + R)
This is the harmonic mean of P and R

Notice Algorithm 2's F1 (0.175) sits much closer to its lower value (recall 0.1) than its higher one (precision 0.7) — that's the harmonic mean actively penalizing the imbalance, exactly the behavior a plain average lacks.

6 Key Takeaway

Threshold is a judgment call; F1 is a comparison tool

Moving the decision threshold is how you personally trade off precision against recall based on the real-world cost of false positives vs. false negatives — this stays a manual, application-specific choice. F1 score is a separate tool: once you have precision/recall numbers for several candidate algorithms (each perhaps at their own best threshold), F1 gives a single number to compare them fairly, without being fooled by a degenerate model that games one metric at the expense of the other.
