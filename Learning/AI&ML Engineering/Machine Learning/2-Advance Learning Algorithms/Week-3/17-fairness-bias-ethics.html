Lesson 17 — Fairness, Bias, and Ethics in ML

No checklist exists for being ethical — but there are concrete practices that make systems meaningfully less biased and more accountable.

1 Real Cases of Unacceptable Bias

✕
Hiring tool shown to discriminate against women — the company stopped using it, but only after it had already been deployed.
✕
Face recognition systems that matched dark-skinned individuals to criminal mugshots far more often than lighter-skinned individuals.
✕
Bank loan approval systems that discriminated against certain subgroups.
✕
Reinforcing negative stereotypes — e.g. a search for a profession that never surfaces anyone who looks like the searcher can discourage them before they even try.
"I have a daughter, and if she searches online for certain professions and doesn't see anyone that looks like her, I would hate for that to discourage her."

2 Beyond Bias: Adverse Use Cases

Deepfakes — a widely-viewed BuzzFeed deepfake of President Obama was released with full transparency and disclosure, which made it acceptable as a demonstration; using this technology without consent or disclosure would not be.
Engagement-optimized toxicity — algorithms tuned purely for engagement can end up amplifying toxic or incendiary content as a side effect.
Bots for fake content — generating fake product reviews or politically-motivated fake content.
Fraud — the same techniques used to fight spam and financial fraud are also used by the spammers and fraudsters themselves — an ongoing arms race.

3 A Personal Line to Draw

"There have been multiple times that I have looked at a project that seemed to be financially sound... but I have killed the project just on ethical grounds, because I felt that it makes the world worse off — and I just don't ever want to be involved in a project like that."

The advice is direct: if asked to work on something you consider unethical, walk away. Financial soundness alone isn't sufficient justification to build something.

4 Why There's No Checklist

Ethics has been studied for millennia without producing a simple, universal algorithm. Attempting to reduce it to "do these 5 things and you're ethical" doesn't work — what follows instead is general guidance, not a guarantee.

5 Practical Guidelines

1
Assemble a diverse team to brainstorm what could go wrong — with particular emphasis on harm to vulnerable groups. Diversity across gender, ethnicity, culture, and other traits was found to meaningfully improve a team's ability to spot problems before rollout.
2
Search the literature for existing standards or guidelines in your industry — e.g. emerging fairness standards in the financial industry for loan-approval systems.
3
Audit the system against the identified dimensions of possible harm, after training but before deployment — e.g. explicitly measure performance across genders or ethnicities if bias there was flagged as a risk.
4
Develop a mitigation plan (e.g. rollback to a known-fair earlier version), and continue monitoring for harm even after deployment so the plan can be triggered quickly if needed.
The audit step sits squarely between "train model" and "deploy in production" — the last checkpoint before real users are affected.

6 Not All Applications Carry Equal Weight

Low ethical stakes

A neural network deciding how long to roast coffee beans — errors here have minimal real-world consequence.

High ethical stakes

A system deciding which bank loans get approved — bias here can cause significant, compounding harm to real people's financial lives.

The level of scrutiny an application deserves should scale with the real-world consequences of it getting things wrong.

7 The Self-Driving Car Example: Mitigation Before Deployment

Plan before, not after

Self-driving car teams developed accident-response mitigation plans before rolling cars out on the road — so that if an accident occurred, there was already a plan to execute immediately, rather than scrambling to figure out what to do after the fact. This is the general pattern worth adopting: plan the mitigation before deployment, not in reaction to a crisis.

8 Key Takeaway

This isn't optional homework

Ethics, fairness, and bias should be treated as seriously as accuracy or performance — not brushed off as secondary concerns. There's no algorithm for being ethical, but diverse brainstorming, industry-standard research, pre-deployment auditing, and a ready mitigation plan are concrete, repeatable practices that meaningfully reduce the odds of shipping something harmful.
