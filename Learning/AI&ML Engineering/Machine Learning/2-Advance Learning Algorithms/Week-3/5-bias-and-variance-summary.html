Lesson 5 — Bias and Variance

The single most useful diagnostic for deciding what to do next when a model isn't performing well.

1 The Three Classic Fits

Familiar from linear regression: fit a straight line, a quadratic, or a 4th-order polynomial to the same housing data.

High Bias (Underfit)
f = w₁x + b
J_train HIGH
J_cv HIGH
"Just Right"
f = w₁x + w₂x² + b
J_train LOW
J_cv LOW
High Variance (Overfit)
f = w₁x + w₂x² + w₃x³ + w₄x⁴ + b
J_train LOW
J_cv HIGH

With one feature, you can just look at the plot to see which is happening. With many features, plotting f(x) isn't possible — so instead you read the same story off two numbers: J_train and J_cv.

2 Reading J_train and J_cv Like a Diagnostic

SignalWhat it meansDiagnosis
J_train is highModel doesn't even fit the data it was trained onHigh Bias (underfitting)
J_cv ≫ J_train (J_train may be low)Does great on seen data, poorly on unseen dataHigh Variance (overfitting)
J_train low, J_cv low (close together)Fits well AND generalizes wellGood fit
J_train is high AND J_cv ≫ J_trainFits poorly overall, and generalizes even worseHigh Bias + High Variance (rare, mostly in neural nets)
Note on the rare combined case

High bias + high variance together is unusual for simple linear models on 1D data, but can happen — e.g. in neural networks — when part of the input space is overfit while another part is underfit simultaneously. The tell is: J_train is high, and J_cv is even worse than that already-high J_train.

3 The Bias/Variance Curve vs. Polynomial Degree

Plotting J_train and J_cv as a function of polynomial degree d (no regularization) reveals the full picture in one image:

J_train keeps dropping as degree increases (more flexibility → better training fit). J_cv dips then rises — a U-shape. The minimum of J_cv marks the "just right" degree.
Left side (low d)

Underfits → J_train high, J_cv high. High bias region.

Middle (right d)

J_cv at its minimum — neither underfitting nor overfitting.

Right side (high d)

Overfits → J_train low, but J_cv rises again. High variance region.

4 Quick Reference: How to Diagnose

High Bias (underfit)

J_train will be high, and J_train ≈ J_cv (both bad, close together).

High Variance (overfit)

J_cv ≫ J_train — the gap between them is the tell, even if J_train itself looks low.

High Bias AND High Variance (rare)

J_train is high, and J_cv ≫ J_train on top of that. Worst of both worlds — mostly seen in neural network applications, not plain linear regression.

5 Key Takeaway

Whenever a model underperforms, the first question to answer is: is this bias, or variance, or both? That answer determines which of the earlier "six usual suspects" (Lesson 1) is actually worth trying — more data and more regularization fix variance; more features, more polynomial terms, and less regularization fix bias.
