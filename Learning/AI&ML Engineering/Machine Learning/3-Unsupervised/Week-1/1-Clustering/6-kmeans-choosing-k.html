❓ Choosing the Value of K

Why K is often ambiguous, the elbow method, and what actually works in practice

K Is Often Genuinely Ambiguous

Show the same data to different people and ask "how many clusters?" — some will say 2, others will say 4, and both can be right. Because clustering is unsupervised, there's no ground-truth label to check against.

Same data — "2 broad groups" (blue) and "4 tighter groups" (green) are both defensible readings

The Elbow Method

Run K-means for K = 1, 2, 3, ... and plot cost J vs. K. J always decreases as K grows — but sometimes it drops sharply up to a point, then flattens. That bend is the "elbow."

Clear elbow — J drops fast then flattens at K=3
No clear elbow — smooth curve, common in practice
⚠️ Don't just pick K to minimize J. J keeps shrinking as K grows — taken to the extreme, K = m (one cluster per point) gives J = 0. That's not useful clustering, just overfitting.

What Actually Works: Downstream Purpose

In practice, clusters exist to serve some later use — so evaluate K by how well it serves that purpose, not by the shape of the J curve.

K=3S / M / L t-shirtsSimpler, cheaper to manufacture & ship — looser fit
K=5XS / S / M / L / XLBetter individual fit — more manufacturing & shipping cost
The "right" K is a business decision (fit quality vs. cost), not something the algorithm can output on its own. Run both, compare, decide.

Another Example: Image Compression

In the practice lab, K = number of colors used to represent a compressed image. Fewer colors (small K) → smaller file, more visible quality loss. More colors (large K) → better image quality, bigger file. Again, K is chosen manually based on the actual trade-off you care about.

💡 Key Takeaways

K is often genuinely ambiguous Elbow method: sometimes useful, often no clear elbow Never pick K just to minimize J Best approach: evaluate K by downstream task performance
