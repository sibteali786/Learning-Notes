PCA: reducing the number of features

After the recommender videos, Ng introduces principal components analysis (PCA): an unsupervised algorithm, commonly used for visualisation, that takes data with many features (10, 50, even thousands) and compresses it to two or three so you can plot it. This video gives the intuition with cars and countries. The next video covers how the algorithm actually works.

1. The problem: you cannot plot 50 dimensions

Dimensions = features

“50-dimensional data” just means 50 features. A screen shows two (or, rotated, three). PCA takes the many features and reduces them to 2 or 3, called z1, z2, z3, which you can plot.

Why bother

  • Visualising a new dataset helps you understand what it looks like.
  • It can reveal that something funny or unexpected is going on in the data.
  • Commonly used by data scientists for exactly this.

2. Three car examples: when can you just drop a feature?

Slide: “Car measurements”. Running example: passenger cars (length, width, wheel diameter, height, and more). The sketches below are my own simple scatterplots of each case.

Case 1

Length + width

Width must fit a road lane, so it barely varies (in the US about 1.8 m, just under six feet). Length varies a lot. Just take x1 (length).

Case 2

Length + wheel diameter

Wheel diameter varies a little, but length dominates. Again just take x1 (the slide says exactly this). PCA would more or less decide this automatically.

Case 3

Length + height

Both vary a lot, and bigger cars tend to be longer and taller. Dropping either loses useful information. This is where PCA does more than pick a feature.

3. The idea: find a new axis (slide “Size”)

  • Instead of choosing the x1 axis or the x2 axis, find a new axis z that lies flat in the same plot. It is a combination of x1 and x2, not a third dimension sticking out of the page.
  • A car’s coordinate on z (the distance along that axis) tells you roughly its size.
  • So two numbers (length, height) become one number (z). Slide: “find new axis and coordinates, use fewer numbers to capture the ‘size’ feature”.
  • Goal: when you measure your data’s coordinates on the new axis, you still have very useful information about the car.
  • The next few videos formalise this.
In this video’s examples: 2 features → 1 feature.
In practice: many features (10, 20, 50, thousands) → 2 or 3 features, so you can plot in 2D or 3D.

Try it: rotate the z-axis (my illustration)

4. From 3D to 2D: the “pancake”

Slide: “From 3D to 2D”.

  • A 3-feature dataset (x1, x2, x3) can be rotated on screen. Seen from the right angle, most points lie on a very thin surface, like a two-dimensional pancake floating in 3D space.
  • PCA replaces the three features with two numbers z1 and z2, the coordinates on that pancake.
  • Then you can plot z1 vs z2 flat, for example printed on paper, where you cannot rotate the 3D view.

  • Possible features: GDP (x1), per capita GDP, Human Development Index (a measure of overall progress based on things like lifespan and education), life expectancy, and so on, up to 50.
  • PCA compresses them to z1 and z2, and you plot each country as one point.

How Ng reads the two new axes

  • z1 ≈ country’s total GDP / how big the economy is (bigger countries have more people and larger economies).
  • z2 ≈ per-person GDP (economic activity per person).
  • Words used: “loosely corresponds”, “roughly”, “perhaps”. PCA does not label the axes. You interpret them.

Reading the scatter plot

  • USA: large country, high per-person activity, so up and to the right.
  • Singapore: smaller country, still high per-person activity, so a lower z1 but high z2.
  • Lower-left: a smaller country with low per-person activity. Lower-right: a large country with low per-person activity.
Check against the table: USA has GDP 14.527 and per capita 46.86. Singapore has GDP 0.223 but per capita 56.69. That is the same pattern the plot shows (USA far right, Singapore left but high).

Real-life scenarios

Cars (from the lecture)

A car dataset has length, width, height, wheel diameter, and dozens more. A 2-number summary lets you see “small city cars” vs “large trucks” as clusters.

Customers (my example)

An online shop records 50 numbers per customer. Plot them in 2D to spot groups of similar customers, or one odd cluster that points to a data problem (the “something funny” case).

Alternatives and trade-offs

My additions, not from the lecture. Details of PCA itself come in the following videos.

ApproachProsCons
Just pick the original features with the most variation (cases 1 and 2)Easy to explain: each axis keeps its real-world meaningFails in case 3, where several features carry information together
PCA (new axes as combinations of features)Handles case 3; automatic; fewer numbers per exampleNew axes are blends, so you must interpret them (like “size”). Mixed feature scales may need attention, which later videos should cover
Other visualisation methods (for example t-SNE, UMAP; not covered here)Can show non-linear structure that a flat PCA plot missesLess simple to explain; results depend more on settings

Key takeaways

  • PCA is an unsupervised algorithm that reduces many features to 2 or 3, mainly to visualise data.
  • If one feature barely varies (width, wheel diameter), you can just drop it. PCA effectively does this automatically.
  • If several features vary together (length and height), PCA finds a new axis z, a combination of them that lies in the same plot. A point’s coordinate on z captures things like “size”.
  • 3D data on a “pancake” becomes 2D (z1, z2). 50 country features become 2 (roughly total GDP and per-person GDP).
  • Plotting helps you understand data and notice anything unexpected.
