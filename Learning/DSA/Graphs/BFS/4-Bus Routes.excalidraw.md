Building stopToBuses — this is actually O(S), not O(B×S).

Look at the nested loop: for row = 0 to buses-1: for col = 0 to routes[row].length-1. This isn't a full B × S grid — col only goes up to that specific bus's stop count, not a fixed S every time. If bus 0 has 3 stops and bus 1 has 5 stops, the total number of iterations across both rows combined is 3 + 5 = 8, which is exactly S (the sum you defined). So the total work across the whole double loop, summed over all rows, is just S — not B × S. The diagram's O(B×S) annotation on that block is loose; the tight bound is O(S).

Now the BFS traversal — this is where B × S genuinely shows up, and here's why.

Each bus is popped from the queue at most once (the visited set guarantees that). When a bus bus is processed, you loop over routes[bus] — summed across all buses ever processed, that's again O(S) total (same reasoning as above — each stop-entry gets touched once across the whole BFS run, not once per bus times every stop).

But for each of those stop visits, you then do neighbors = stopToBuses[stop] and loop over neighbors — and in the worst case, how many buses can share one stop? Up to B (every bus could theoretically pass through the same stop). So this inner neighbor-loop can do up to B work, for each of the S stop-visits across the whole run.

That gives total neighbor-scanning work of O(S × B) — this is the dominant term, and it's where the real B × S bound comes from, not the initial map-building step.

So, total time complexity: O(B × S) — driven by the neighbor-scanning phase of BFS, with O(S) map-building folding in as a lower-order term. ^LUswtBwa

Space: O(S + B) — stopToBuses holds O(S) total entries across all its arrays combined (each stop-entry contributes exactly one array slot somewhere, no matter how the sharing is distributed), and visited/queue are each bounded by O(B) since a bus enters either at most once.

Final complexity for Bus Routes:

Time: O(B × S)
Space: O(S + B) ^qGVnbl2Y
